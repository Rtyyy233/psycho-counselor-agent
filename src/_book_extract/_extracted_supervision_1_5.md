# CHAPTER 1: THE PRACTICE OF CLINICAL SUPERVISION

## 1.1 Definition of Supervision

Supervision is defined as:

> A distinct professional activity in which education and training aimed at developing science-informed practice are facilitated through a collaborative interpersonal process. It involves observation, evaluation, feedback, the facilitation of supervisee self-assessment, and the acquisition of knowledge and skills by instruction, modeling, and mutual problem solving. In addition, by building on the recognition of the strengths and talents of the supervisee, supervision encourages self-efficacy. Supervision ensures that clinical consultation is conducted in a competent manner in which ethical standards, legal prescriptions, and professional practices are used to promote and protect the welfare of the client, the profession, and society at large.

Supervision serves two essential and interrelated functions:
1. To ensure the integrity of clinical services provided to the client (quality assurance)
2. To develop competence in the supervisee

Quality assurance is the **primary ethical responsibility** of the supervisor and supersedes educative, training, and evaluative functions.

## 1.2 The Three Pillars of Supervision

Effective supervision builds on three interrelated pillars, each relying on and contributing to the others through a synergistic confluence:

### Pillar 1: The Supervisory Relationship
- Provides the foundation for the development of an alliance
- Individual and shared responsibilities in supervision are fulfilled through it
- The supervisory alliance is, to a great extent, the result of the nature and quality of the relationship formed between supervisor and supervisee

### Pillar 2: Inquiry
- Refers to all processes that facilitate critical understanding of the therapeutic process
- Fosters in the supervisee greater awareness of his or her professional and personal contributions to therapy
- Involves modeling inquiry as a process of exploration (not criticism)

### Pillar 3: Educational Praxis
- Provides multiple learning strategies tailored to enhance supervisee's knowledge and technical skills
- Includes instruction, observation, role playing, and other methods
- Represents the assemblage of professional competencies, interpersonal skills, personal strengths, and values

## 1.3 Four Superordinate Values

### Value 1: Integrity-in-Relationship
- Integrity refers to a state of completeness as well as moral incorruptibility
- Any corruption (boundary violations, inattention to professional responsibilities) compromises integrity
- Requires active commitment to the *complete* use of supervision (considering ALL factors)
- Contributes to development of a collaborative, trusting, supportive supervisory alliance
- Subtle affirmations or detractions influence full development of integrity (e.g., consistently being on time, fully attending to supervisee's exploration rather than simply offering a suggestion)

### Value 2: Ethical Values-Based Practice
- Psychology is a human enterprise in which faith commitments and values shape methods of inquiry and clinical practice
- Supervisors model professional values, principles, and ethics
- Acts of encouraging hope or challenging a client to self-responsibility are moral acts as well as technical interventions
- Exploration of values is relevant to training (not the same as providing psychotherapy)
- Ethics and competence are yoked: ethics demands competence, and competence requires ethical practice
- Clinical practice in every instance involves the application of ethics

### Value 3: Appreciation of Diversity
- Expression of the profession's respect for people's rights and dignity (APA Principle E)
- Involves "deconstruction of the inescapable framework" of beliefs, assumptions, and morality
- Requires antiobjectivist stance: recognizing that identity is socially constructed and privileged by the culture into which one is born
- All people are "multicultural beings," all interactions are cross-cultural
- Diversity competency includes: self-awareness by both supervisor and supervisee; awareness, knowledge, and appreciation of interactions among client's, supervisee's, and supervisor's assumptions, values, biases, expectations, and worldviews; integrating and practicing appropriate, relevant, and sensitive assessment and intervention strategies; accounting for larger milieu of organization, society, and sociopolitical variables

### Value 4: Science-Informed Practice
- Integration of science with practice is a defining characteristic of the profession
- Supervisors teach and demonstrate how knowledge and practices derived from science are systematically applied
- Active use of scientific literature, initiating/participating in applied research projects
- Instilling "the questioning attitude of the scientist" (Shakow, 1976) — skills in observation, critical thinking, and hypothesis testing

## 1.4 Definition of Competence

Based on Epstein and Hundert (2002):

> "The habitual and judicious use of communication, knowledge, technical skills, clinical reasoning, emotions, values, and reflection in daily practice for the benefit of the individual and the community being served."

Key characteristics:
- Competence is the state of **sufficiency** relative to specific performance or training requirements within a given setting
- Competence is NOT an absolute; it reflects sufficiency of a broad spectrum of personal and professional abilities relative to a given requirement
- Input-model approaches: define educational curricula necessary to become a psychologist
- Output-model approaches: emphasize particular roles and functions a psychologist should perform
- Competencies consist of unique assemblages of discrete clinical abilities incorporating specific knowledge, skills, values, emotions required to perform a specific professional activity
- Competence measure for a graduate student is NOT the same as for a licensed practitioner or expert

## 1.5 Outcomes of Supervision

Primary outcomes:
1. **Quality assurance** — monitoring supervisee's conduct to ensure appropriate and ethical professional practices (primary ethical responsibility)
2. **Competence development** — learning how to apply knowledge, theory, and clinical procedures to solve human problems
3. **Socialization to the profession** — professional values, commitments, and identity formation; career goal formulation
4. **Intermediate outcomes** — increases in role assimilation, self-assessment, and self-efficacy
5. **Gatekeeper function** — formal evaluation and verification of supervisee's competence and readiness for next training level
6. **Tacit training in supervision** — supervisee's experiences form basis for their own future conduct of supervision
7. **Consultation with academic programs** — supervisor provides feedback ensuring adequate preparation of beginning practitioners

Formative and summative feedback is provided through the formal structure of the training agreement/contract. Summative evaluations are typically conducted at midpoint and end of rotation.

## 1.6 Approaches to Supervision

### Classification System
Approaches classified according to whether they are:
1. Extensions of psychotherapy theory (psychotherapy-based)
2. Developed specifically for supervision (process-based approaches)
3. Developmental models (descriptive rather than prescriptive)

### 1.6.1 Psychotherapy-Based Approaches

Supervision originally incorporated clinical approaches and techniques from major theoretical orientations:

- **Psychodynamic** (Binder & Strupp, 1997b; Dewald, 1987, 1997; Ekstein & Wallerstein, 1972; Frawley-O'Dea & Sarnat, 2001; Rock, 1997)
- **Cognitive and Cognitive-Behavioral** (Friedberg & Taylor, 1994; Fruzzetti, Waltz, & Linehan, 1997; Liese & Beck, 1997; Milne & James, 2000; Ferris, 1994; Rosenbaum & Ronen, 1998; Woods & Ellis, 1997)
- **Client-Centered and Existential-Humanistic** (Mahrer & Boulet, 1997; Patterson, 1997; Sterling & Bugental, 1993)
- **Intersubjective** (Bob, 1999)
- **Systemic and Family Systems** (Breunlin, Rampage, & Eovaldi, 1995; Liddle, Becker, & Diamond, 1997; Liddle, Breunlin, & Schwartz, 1988; Storm, Todd, Sprenkle, & Morgan, 2001)

**How psychotherapy-based approaches are incorporated:**
- Theoretical orientation informs observation and selection of clinical data, and meanings/relevance of those data
- Observable cognitions and behaviors are primary for cognitive-behavioral therapists
- Affective reactions and subjective experiences are of interest to psychodynamic clinicians
- Skills associated with specific theory-based interventions shape training focus
- REBT supervisors encourage supervisees to be "active-directive persuasive teachers" and monitor/dispute their own irrationalities
- Systemic supervisors consider roles and functions of entire system
- Narrative approach (intersubjective) focuses on dialogue exploring constructed realities
- Cognitive-behavioral techniques (agenda setting, homework, capsule summaries) are directly applied in supervision

**Advantage:** Clinical techniques may be demonstrated and modeled within supervisory relationship dynamics; seamless consistency of conceptualization

**Limitation:** Aims of supervision are not equivalent to those of psychotherapy; approaches must be specifically tailored to accomplish goals of clinical oversight and training

### 1.6.2 Developmental Approaches

Developmental models are **metatheoretical** and applicable to varying theoretical persuasions. Each presents a developmental sequence from novice to seasoned/master therapist.

**Stage models identified by various authors:**
- Stagnation, confusion, and integration (Loganbill, Hardy, & Delworth, 1982)
- Excitement and anticipatory anxiety; dependency and identification; exuberance and taking charge; identity and independence (Friedman & Kaslow, 1986)
- Skill-based progression (Grater, 1985)
- Emotional characteristics (Friedman & Kaslow, 1986)
- Awareness, motivation, and autonomy (Stoltenberg et al., 1998)

### Integrated Developmental Model (IDM) — Stoltenberg, McNeill, & Delworth (1998)

The most comprehensive model available. Considers trainee's: cognitive and affective awareness of the client; motivation (perceived efforts, enthusiasm, investment across time); autonomy (individuation, independence).

Development is **domain specific** and occurs at different times and rates across eight domains.

#### Level-1 Supervisee
**Characteristics:**
- Exhibits high anxiety
- Exhibits high motivation
- Is dependent on the supervisor
- Focuses predominantly on the self, particularly on performance of technique or following of guidelines
- Is fearful of evaluation

**Supervisor of Level-1 Supervisee should:**
- Be supportive and prescriptive
- Provide structure and positive feedback
- Use minimal direct confrontation
- Have the supervisee work with only mildly distressed clients
- Institute observation and role-play
- Put theory on the back burner to emphasize and encourage conceptualization, skill acquisition and development, self-monitoring of skill development, and attention to the client response to therapeutic intervention

**Note:** Level-2 supervisees can be excellent Level-1 supervisors.

**Methods to Foster Transition from Level 1 to Level 2:**
- Encourage increased autonomy
- Begin reduction in structure
- Encourage new techniques rather than familiar ones only
- Foster focus on the client and his or her reactions and process

#### Level-2 Supervisee
**Characteristics:**
- Is going through "trial and tribulation" period
- Experiences dependency-autonomy conflicts
- Has fluctuating confidence and motivation levels
- Shifts focus to the client, with increased empathy
- Links mood to success with clients
- Has an increased understanding of own limitations
- Uses therapeutic self in interventions
- Demonstrates uneven theoretical and conceptual integration
- Is sensitive and anxious about evaluation

**Supervisor of Level-2 Supervisee should:**
- Balance the fostering of autonomy with support and structure
- Desire to increase autonomy and confidence within realistic boundaries of competence
- Introduce and consider countertransference
- Deal with self, defensiveness, transference, affect, and the supervisory relationship
- Articulate theory and conceptual frames
- Challenge and use catalytic interventions, stirring up and increasing awareness
- Help the supervisee identify and understand his or her strengths and weaknesses
- Accept deidealization of him- or herself
- Monitor use of videotapes and direct observation
- Promote multiple theoretical conceptualizations of the same material

#### Level-3 Supervisee
**Characteristics:**
- Exudes an attitude of "calm after the storm"
- Exhibits stable motivation
- Is secure with autonomy
- Focuses on the client, process, and self
- Has professional identity at the core of his or her treatment
- Is not disabled by remaining doubts
- Accepts own strengths and weaknesses
- Exhibits high empathy and understanding
- Uses the therapeutic self in interventions
- Integrates client information, personal responses, theoretical information, and empirical information
- May find it a challenge to be flexible in approach
- Has accurate empathy that is tempered by objectivity and processing of reactions, feelings, and thoughts
- Has a high level of insight into personal strengths and weaknesses
- Addresses areas of weakness with increased confidence and nondefensiveness

**Supervisor of Level-3 Supervisee should:**
- Carefully assess consistency in performance areas across domains
- Work towards integration across domains
- Continue careful monitoring
- Emphasize autonomy and growth
- Avoid creating an intrusive and overly structured supervisory environment
- Create a supportive supervisory environment
- Engage in confrontation
- Devote attention to parallel process, countertransference, and the supervisor-supervisee relationship
- Focus on leading the supervisee to make personal self-discoveries
- Interact with empathy and in a nonjudgmental manner, with encouragement of experimentation and exploration
- Provide advice concerning professional development and job searches as needed

**Methods to Foster Transition from Level 2 to Level 3:**
- Encourage movement towards stable motivation across domains
- Foster flexibility to autonomously move conceptually and behaviorally across domains
- Encourage creation of a solid professional identity
- Encourage development of a personalized understanding across domains
- Encourage assessment of the impact of personal events on professional life

**Assessment tool:** The Supervisee Levels Questionnaire (McNeill, Stoltenberg, & Romans, 1992)

### Supervisor Development Theories

Theories share characteristics: metatheoretical, sequential, progress to a fixed endpoint.

#### Watkins (1993) — Four Primary Issues/Dimensions:
1. **Competency-incompetency** — degree to which one feels competent to perform as a supervisor
2. **Autonomy-dependency** — degree of independence with which one performs
3. **Identity-identity diffusion** — degree of identification vs. confusion with supervisory role
4. **Self-awareness-unawareness** — degree of awareness concerning the role and process of supervision

**Watkins' Stages:**
- **Stage 1 — Role Shock:** experience of "imposter phenomenon"; awareness of weaknesses; lack of identity as supervisor; feeling of being overwhelmed; sensitivity to feedback; may withdraw or "attack" supervisees
- **Stage 2 — Recovery and Transition:** first steps towards supervisory identity formation; strengths recognized; confidence increases; identity begins to form; more realistic appraisal of self
- **Stage 3 — Role Consolidation:** increased confidence; more realistic appraisals of skills; more completely developed sense of identity; transference and countertransference are identified

#### Hess (1986, 1987b) — Three Stages:
- **Stage 1 — Beginning:** movement from supervisee to supervisor; lack of awareness of issues; sensitivity to feedback; self-consciousness; focus on teaching
- **Stage 2 — Exploration:** feelings of greater confidence; greater effectiveness; stronger ability to evaluate work; may be too intrusive or restrictive in roles employed
- **Stage 3 — Confirmation of Supervisory Identity:** strong sense of identity; excitement about improvement; less work and more relationship; increased focus on learning agenda

#### Stoltenberg et al. (1998) — Supervisor Levels:
- **Level 1:** Strong expert roles; reliance on recent supervisee as models; high anxiety; preference for structured feedback format
- **Level 2:** Still high levels of confusion and conflict; fluctuating motivation; overfocus on supervisee or loss of focus; temptation to provide therapy to trainees; continuation of search for comfort areas
- **Level 3:** Stable and consistent motivation

#### Rodenhauser (1994) — Three Stages:
- **Stage I — Unconscious Identification:** with or unconscious emulation of one's former supervisor
- **Stage II — Conceptualization:** a personal search for concepts
- **Stage III — Incorporation:** learning supervision is a relationship

#### Additional Stage Models:
- **Stage 1:** Establishment and definition of supervisory relationship; supervisory role is used to facilitate goal setting
- **Stage 2:** Supervisor takes responsibility for structuring and managing supervision sessions; roles are teacher and counselor
- **Stage 3:** Responsibility for structuring supervision shifts to trainee; self-evaluation is encouraged; supervisor acts as a consultant

**Authors' observation on novice supervisor:** Significant entry-role insecurity manifested in rigidity; crisis orientation; personalization of trainee performance, errors, and feedback; flip-flopping between being unforgiving, enthusiastic, and oversupervising.

**Authors' view on final phase:** There is NO final phase of supervisor development. Even the most senior supervisor continues to evolve and develop.

**Multicultural developmental models** have also been proposed (Carney & Kahn, 1984; D'Andrea & Daniels, 1997; Leong & Wagner, 1994) and for racial consciousness identity (Helms, 1990; Sabnani, Ponterotto, & Borodovsky, 1991).

**Research on developmental approaches:** Empirical support has NOT been obtained. Ellis and Ladany (1997) concluded that "the overall quality of research during the past 15 years was substandard" and that "theories and the central premises of these remain untested."

**Research findings on supervisor behaviors by experience:**
- Experienced supervisors generated more planning statements (Stone, 1980)
- Experienced supervisors more verbal, more self-disclosing, provided more direct instruction (Marikis, Russell, & Dell, 1985)
- Experienced supervisors used humor more frequently (E.L. Worthington, 1984a)
- Experienced supervisors less likely to project negative personal attributes onto supervisees experiencing difficulty; more likely to attribute problems to situational variables (E.L. Worthington, 1984b)
- Novices attended to superficial detail; experienced clinicians observed abstract and therapeutically relevant dimensions (Kivlighan & Quigley, 1991)
- Act of supervising does NOT increase proficiency; improvement occurs through specific training and increased cognitive complexity (Ellis & Dell, 1986)

### 1.6.3 Process-Based Approaches

Also referred to as social role supervision models. Developed to provide descriptions of component roles, tasks, and processes and to uniformly classify events in supervision. Aim to provide reliable and valid procedures for studying the supervision process.

#### The Discrimination Model (J.M. Bernard, 1997)

Designed as "the simplest of maps" for supervisors-in-training.

**Three Focus Areas:**
1. **Process (or intervention skills)** — all behaviors that distinguish counseling as a purposeful therapeutic interpersonal activity
2. **Conceptualization skills** — trainee's ability to make sense of information the client presents and to consider a response
3. **Personalization skills** — all features of the trainee as an individual that contribute to the therapeutic process (personality, culture, etc.)
- Lanning (1986) proposed a fourth category, **professional behavior**, though Bernard argued it can be subsumed

**Three Supervisor Roles:**
1. **Teacher role** — supervisor determines what is required for trainee to become more competent
2. **Counselor role** — supervisor facilitates exploration of interpersonal or intrapersonal reality of the trainee
3. **Consultant role** — supervisee serves as a resource; encourages trainee to trust own thoughts, insights, and feelings

**Application:** Identifies preferred supervisory roles; evaluates congruence between supervisee's needs and supervisor's responses. Example of incongruence: trainee needs help in case conceptualization and supervisor provides a diagnosis rather than facilitating conceptualization.

#### Systems Approach to Supervision (SAS) — Holloway (1995)

Applies principles of a systems approach emphasizing a learning alliance based on multiple interlinking factors.

**Seven Interrelated Factors:**
1. **The supervision relationship** (CORE FACTOR) — interpersonal structure (dimensions of power and involvement), phases of the relationship, and the supervisory contract
2. The client
3. The supervisor
4. The trainee
5. The institution
6. The functions of supervision
7. The tasks of supervision

**Key emphasis:** Mutuality of the interaction and importance of participants' shared meaning. Focuses on unique needs of the supervisee as negotiated within the relationship (contrasts with developmental approaches which assume levels).

#### Other Process Models:
- **Ecological-behavioral model** (Kratochwill, Lepage, & McGivern, 1997)
- **Microcounseling** (Daniels, Rigazio-Digilio, & Ivey, 1997)
- **Experiential learning model** (Milne & James, 2002)

### 1.6.4 Present Status of Supervision Practice

- Supervision formally identified as a competency only in the 1990s
- Most psychologists have NOT received formal training in supervision
- 42% of supervisors received supervisory training in doctoral programs; 25% in internship (Rodolfa et al., 1998)
- ASPPB Task Force concluded it is "surprising that organized psychology has failed to establish graduate level training in supervision"

## 1.7 Competency-Based Approach to Clinical Supervision

### Definition
Provides an explicit framework and method to initiate, develop, implement, and evaluate the processes and outcomes of supervision. Places emphasis on ability to apply knowledge and skills in the real world and uses performance outcomes as criteria.

### The Conceptual Learning Model (Figure 1.1)
Three domains in developmental trajectory:
1. **Foundation:** Personal traits, values, and interpersonal competencies
2. **Acquired Skills, Abilities and Knowledge:** Graduate education contributes knowledge, skills, and values that inaugurate professional socialization
3. **Assessment Developed in the Learning Process:** Clinical training provides integrative learning experiences; skills, abilities, knowledge, and values interact to form learning bundles that become clinical competencies
4. **Summative Assessment of Performance:** Formal evaluation

### Procedures in Competency-Based Supervision

**Step 1 — Establishing Supervision Goals and Objectives:**
- Identify competencies/input that will be focus of training
- Faculty considers clinical service requirements of setting and reviews literature
- Competencies should directly relate to real-world job requirements
- **Disassembly of competency** into definable and potentially measurable units
- Individual assessment and collaborative goal setting (NOT assigning goals based on assumed developmental levels)
- Skill acquisition is heterogeneous and discontinuous
- Training objectives, learning processes, and modes of assessment included in formal supervision contract

**Step 2 — Case Management and Learning Process:**
- Supervisees begin providing clinical services, drawing on prior education and on-site instruction
- Supervisor assists supervisee to become observant and reflect on therapeutic interaction and contributions to it
- Progresses to collaborative inquiry — both describe observations and discuss what occurred
- Facilitates nondefensive inquiry and encourages self-reflective assessment
- Supervisors determine sufficiency of supervisee's abilities with respect to client needs

### Experiential Learning Model (Milne & James, 2002)
Drawing on Kolb's (1984) model, the learning cycle includes:
1. **Experiencing** — providing the clinical service
2. **Reflecting** — on the experience
3. **Conceptualizing** — understanding what happened
4. **Planning** — what needs to happen next
5. **Experimenting** — performing new behaviors or applying new understanding

The supervisor assists the learner "to go round the experiential learning cycle" using educational methods: didactic teaching, role-playing, and modeling.

### PETS (Process Evaluation of Teaching and Supervision) — Table 1.2
Observable instrument coding supervisor behaviors:

| Behavior | Description | Examples |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| Organizing/Managing | Structuring session flow | "I would like to cover X today" |
| Listening/Observing | Active listening, silent attention | Neither verbal nor nonverbal feedback |
| Supporting/Encouraging | Verbal/nonverbal reassurance | "That's interesting," nodding, smiling |
| Summarizing | Clarifying links and understanding | "Let me see if I got that right" |
| Giving Feedback | Specific verbal/written positive or negative feedback | "The way you set that up was very good" |
| Gathering Information | Asking for facts | "How old is she?" |
| Checking Knowledge | Explicitly monitoring/evaluating competence | "What would you do here?" |
| Challenging | Getting learner to rethink/reason | "How else could you have handled it?" |
| Informing/Directing | Providing abstract data, didactic approach | "The way to approach this is..." |
| Demonstrating/Experiential | Active competence development | Modeling, role-play exercises, learning tasks |
| Self-Disclosing | Revealing something new about self | "Those cases are difficult to handle" |
| Disagreeing | Expressing negative reaction | Making verbal corrections |
| Case Example | Using case from own experience | Case example from trainees |
| Other | Off-task behavior | Social chat, setting up equipment |

### Formative and Summative Evaluations

**Formative evaluations:**
- Continuous, using both formal and informal procedures
- Ongoing inquiry and critique focusing on supervisee's effectiveness
- Instruments and observation procedures provide reliable, valid, useful feedback
- Include consideration of supervisee's ability to reflect on therapeutic process
- Collaborative and continuous during integrative learning phase
- Reinforce value of consistent self-assessment and external feedback

**Summative evaluations:**
- More formal process
- Ensure supervisor's accountability to supervisee, academic/clinical programs, profession, and public
- Ratings of performance culled over time
- Represent supervisee's sufficiency in a given competency
- Usually conducted at midpoint and end of rotation
- Become part of the training record

**Evaluation enhancement:** Provide comprehensive feedback including descriptions of strengths as well as shortcomings. Draw on Seligman's (2002) 24 strengths (curiosity, love of learning, open-mindedness, perseverance, honesty, fairness).

**Self-assessment development:** Reflective self-assessment complements scientific attitude. Encourage "reflection-in-action" (Schon, 1983, 1987, 1995). Facilitate metacognition — awareness into processes by which understanding is constructed. Hoshmand (1994) suggests allowing supervisee to think aloud, identify critical choice points, reflect on rationale, evaluate fit with context, and consider alternatives.

# CHAPTER 2: WHAT MAKES FOR GOOD SUPERVISION?

## 2.1 Best Supervisor Characteristics

### Supportive Relationship Qualities
- **Empathy** — understanding the difficult struggles and self-analysis through which the supervisee must go
- **Warmth and understanding** (Hutt, Scott, & King, 1983; Martin, Goodyear & Newton, 1987)
- **Validation or affirmation** (Wulf & Nelson, 2000)
- **Acceptance** (Hutt et al., 1983)
- **Approachability and attentiveness** (Henderson et al., 1999)
- **Respect for personal integrity, autonomy, and strengths** (Henderson et al., 1999; Hutt et al., 1983; Heppner & Roehlke, 1984)
- **Nonjudgmental stance**
- **Flexibility, genuineness** (Carifio & Hess, 1987; Nelson, 1978)
- **Sense of humor** and use of it in supervision (E.L. Worthington, 1984a)
- **Interest** in supervision (overrides experience and knowledge; Nelson, 1978)

### Competence in Facilitating Learning
- Knowledge and experience relevant to supervisee
- Open-minded approach allowing maximal autonomy
- Specialized expertise (Allen, Szollos, & Williams, 1986; McCarthy et al., 1994)
- Skill (Hutt et al., 1983)
- Theoretical, technical, and conceptual knowledge (Watkins, 1995b)
- Significant skill at analyzing events of therapy (Henderson et al., 1999)
- Encouragement of exploration and experimentation (Worthen & McNeill, 1996)
- Encouragement of supervisee disclosures of actions, feelings, attitudes, conflicts (Hutt et al., 1983; McCarthy et al., 1994)
- Understanding of personal supervisee characteristics and dynamics of supervisory relationship (Gandolfo & Brown, 1987)
- Knowing when to self-disclose to normalize trainee's experience (Worthen & McNeill, 1996)

### Evaluation Practices
- Constructive criticism balanced with encouragement
- Regular feedback (Henderson et al., 1999)
- Articulation of shared expectations and mutual goals (Leddick & Dye, 1987)
- Constructive confrontation (Gandolfo & Brown, 1987; Henderson et al., 1999; C.D. Miller & Getting, 1966)

### Theoretical Grounding
- Working within a cohesive theory (Allen et al., 1986)
- Shared theoretical framework (Kennard, Stewart, & Gluck, 1987)
- Congruent theories associated with greater client improvement (Steinhelber et al., 1984)
- Supervisory emphasis on personal growth valued more than technical-skills orientation (Allen et al., 1986)
- Best supervisors cultivated sense of accomplishment, imagination, respect, inner harmony, wisdom (P.D. Guest & Beutler, 1988)

### Supervisory Styles (Friedlander & Ward, 1984)
Three metastyle categories:
1. **Attractive** — friendly, warm, trusting, open, flexible (positive and supportive)
2. **Interpersonally sensitive** — invested, committed, reflective, creative, resourceful (perceptive and intuitive)
3. **Task oriented** — goal-oriented, didactic, prescriptive, thorough, evaluative, practical, concrete

**Orientation correlations:** Cognitive-behavioral supervisors had higher task-oriented ratings; psychodynamic/humanistic supervisors rated more interpersonally sensitive and less task directed.

### Style Preferences by Theory:
- Behavioral interns preferred task-oriented supervisors
- Psychodynamic/humanistic interns preferred warm, supportive, friendly supervisors

### Most Effective Styles (Cherniss & Equatios, 1977):
1. **Insight-oriented** — supervisor asks questions activating supervisee to solve problems independently
2. **Feelings-oriented** — supervisor encourages supervisee to question and deal with emotional responses to clinical process
3. **Didactic-consultative** — supervisor offers advice, suggestions, interpretations concerning client dynamics and clinical technique (most prevalent in community mental health)
- Laissez-faire approach negatively associated with satisfaction
- Ideal supervisor combines multiple styles with higher frequencies of insight-oriented, feelings-oriented, and didactic-consultative

## 2.2 The Supervisory Relationship

### Bidirectional qualities:
- **Bidirectional trust, respect, and facilitation** were hallmarks of a strong supervisory relationship
- **Committing enthusiasm and energy** to supervision was critical
- **Sanctity of the time commitment**
- **Sensitivity to developmental needs**
- **Encouragement of autonomy**
- **Disclosure of discomfort** — comfort in disclosure of perceived errors by either member; responsibility for identifying discomfort/conflict lies with the supervisor
- **Confrontation** is a desirable component
- **Clarity of expectations, regular feedback, and evaluation mechanisms**

### Working Alliance (Bordin, 1983)
Consists of three components:
1. **Bond** — emotional connection including trust, respect, caring
2. **Agreement on goals**
3. **Agreement on tasks**

Strong emotional bond associated with less role conflict and role ambiguity (resolves conflicts more readily). Greater supervisor self-disclosure associated with stronger supervisory working alliance.

## 2.3 Supervisees Whom Supervisors Prefer

### Characteristics of effective supervisees (Vespia, Heckman-Stone, & Delworth, 2002):
- Beginning supervisees ranked "asks for help when appropriate" highest
- Advanced supervisees ranked "demonstrates a willingness to grow" highest
- Interns ranked "actively participates in supervision sessions" highest
- Supervisors ranked "implements supervisor's directives when client welfare is of concern to supervisor" highest

### Kauderer and Herron (1990):
Supervisors viewed most positively: less dependent, assertive trainees who actively participated and limited self-exploration (respecting the boundary between supervision and therapy).

### Henderson et al. (1999) — Trainee abilities valued by supervisors:
- Ability to integrate what learned in class with conceptualization and therapy
- Ability to be aware of own emotional response to clients as it happens
- Ability to use own response in therapy
- Ability to form relationships with clients, peers, and supervisors
- Flexibility
- Adherence to ethical codes and standards

## 2.4 Worst Supervisor Characteristics

### Magnuson et al. (2000) — "Lousy Supervision" Principles:
1. **Unbalanced supervision** — does not represent all elements; focuses on detail to exclusion of larger themes
2. **Developmentally inappropriate supervision** — not sensitive to individual needs; intolerant of differences; does not allow trainee separate views or styles
3. **Poor modeling** of professional and personal attributes — untrained or poorly prepared; professionally apathetic, lazy, uncommitted

### Three spheres of lousy supervision:
1. **Organizational-administrative** — expectations and standards of accountability unclear; supervisee needs not assessed
2. **Technical-cognitive** — supervisor viewed as unskilled as clinician and supervisor; unreliable as professional resource
3. **Relational-affective** — did not provide safe environment; too little or too much affirming/corrective feedback; insensitive to developmental needs; imposed personal agenda; avoided issues between supervisor and supervisee

### Additional worst supervisor behaviors:
- Unavailable or lacked time for supervision (McCarthy et al., 1994)
- Disagreed theoretically or conceptually with supervisees
- Too nondirective or vague
- Personality conflicts
- Spent too much time on administrative issues
- Lacked expertise
- Discussed own work too much
- Canceled or interrupted sessions; seemed distracted (Chung, Baskin, & Case, 1998)
- Preoccupied with personal problems (Allen et al., 1986; Ladany & Walker, 2003)
- Authoritarian — encouraging conformity and punishing divergence from "party line"
- Emphasized shortcomings; inflexible and intolerant (Watkins, 1997c)
- Demeaned the supervisee (Allen et al., 1986)
- Indirect or avoidant; did not foster individuation (Wulf & Nelson, 2000)
- Defensive and uninterested in training to improve supervisory skills (Watkins, 1997c)

### Kadushin's (1968) "Games" — Problematic Interactional Patterns:
1. Supervisee flatters supervisor with praise of brilliant insights → supervisor cannot give corrective feedback
2. Supervisee redefines relationship into friendship (coffee, lunch)
3. Supervisee alludes to knowledge unknown to supervisor → changes power differential → collusion
4. Supervisee alludes to personal/professional experiences placing them in expert position
5. Supervisee plies supervisor with extensive lists of questions → supervision deflected from case discussion
6. Supervisee casts supervisor as capable knowing parent → dependent on advice
7. Supervisee scrupulously follows suggestions then reports they didn't work → supervisor defends position
8. Supervisee expresses confusion over contradictory input from several supervisors → supervisor becomes defensive
9. Supervisee flagellates self about perceived mistakes → supervisor must support/reassure → avoids discussing errors
10. Supervisee raises personal problems → invites supervisor to solve → boundary crossing into treatment
11. Supervisee disagrees → supervisor redefines as psychological defense
12. Supervisee poses questions → supervisor responds with other questions
13. Supervisor-initiated: supervisor redefines supervisee questions/disagreement as resistance

**Resolution:** Address each pattern in supervisor-supervisee seminars; label and deal with directly when they arise.

### Negative Supervision (Nelson & Friedlander, 2001):
Three categories:
1. **Initiation of the relationship** — supervisors remote and uncommitted; too busy
2. **Impasse characteristics** — power struggles; role conflicts; role/boundary conflicts (supervisor attempting to be friend, confide in supervisee, use as sounding board); misunderstandings based on gender/culture worldview differences
3. **Contributing factors** — supervisees made privy to internecine quarrels within agency

### Counterproductive Events (Gray, Ladany, Walker, & Ancis, 2001):
- Supervisor dismissing supervisee's conceptualizations in favor of own
- Inappropriate self-disclosure
- Being unprepared for supervision
- Misunderstanding the supervisee
- Focusing on negative instances rather than areas of improvement
- Supervisee responses: negative thoughts about self, supervisor, or relationship; deferring to supervisor; guarded/hypervigilant stance; censoring/limiting expression; disengagement; **spurious compliance** (pretending to do what supervisor suggested, distorting process notes, concealing difficulties)

## 2.5 Conflict Resolution

**Moskowitz and Rupert (1983) findings:**
- 38% of sample reported major conflict with a supervisor
- Conflicts over style (direction/support) were more readily resolved successfully
- Conflicts over theoretical orientation/therapeutic approach were more difficult and less successful
- **Most difficult and most frequent:** conflicts involving personality issues
- **86% preferred supervisor identify and initiate discussion** of conflict
- Only 13.9% preferred supervisor identify and wait for trainee to initiate
- 40 of 52 practicum students initiated discussion → led to improvement in over half of cases
- When discussion didn't lead to improvement: supervisor didn't change behavior, or supervisor felt trainee's personal problem was the crux

**Critical competency:** Respond nondefensively to supervisees' negative feedback or highlighting of areas of disagreement. Practicing nondefensive response is excellent role-play activity for supervisory seminars.

## 2.6 Disclosure

### Supervisee Nondisclosure (Ladany, Hill, Corbett, & Nutt, 1996):
- Average of 8 nondisclosures to supervisors
- Issues of moderate importance
- Most often: negative reactions to supervisors, personal issues, evaluation concerns, clinical mistakes, general clinical observations
- **90% experienced negative reaction to supervisor they did not disclose**
- Reasons: viewed information as unimportant, too personal, feelings too negative, feared alliance not strong
- 39.8% failed to inform supervisors of perceived clinical errors (moderate to high frequency)
- 30% never or infrequently disclosed when they thought supervisor was wrong
- Nearly 50% told supervisor what he/she wanted to hear
- 59% never or infrequently felt comfortable disclosing negative feelings toward supervisor
- Only protective factors: discussion of countertransference and supervisee satisfaction

### Supervisor Self-Disclosure (Ladany & Lehrman-Waterman, 1999):
- 91% of trainees reported at least one self-disclosure from supervisor
- Most frequent: personal issues, neutral counseling experiences, counseling struggles
- 73% of supervisors made at least one personal self-disclosure
- **Facilitative self-disclosures:** emotional reactions to clients, counseling struggles/successes, personal feedback on supervisory relationship, general professional experiences, didactic mentoring providing vicarious experiences
- Risk: time lost to supervision, placing needs over supervisee, role reversal

### Supervisor Nondisclosure (Ladany & Melincoff, 1999):
- **98% of supervisors** withheld information from trainees
- Most often (74%): negative reaction to supervisee's professional performance
- Why not disclosed: perceived developmental process into which behavior fit; trainee not ready
- Sometimes addressed issue less directly and more generally
- Full breakdown of nondisclosure types: negative reactions to trainee performance (56%), trainee personal issues (37%), negative self-efficacy of supervisor (32%), dynamics of training site (27%), supervisor's clinical/professional issues (22%), trainee appearance (18%), positive reactions to trainee's counseling (11%), attraction to trainee (10%), reactions to trainee's clients (4%), experiences with other trainees (4%)

## 2.7 Supervision Formats

### Ranking by Preference (Goodyear & Nelson, 1997)
**Highest rated:**
1. Supervisor and trainee review videotapes of therapy sessions
2. Live supervision using phone call-ins to direct the trainee
3. Supervisor conducts co-therapy with trainee / Trainees participate on treatment team behind one-way mirror
4. Live supervision using a mid-session break to consult with trainee
5. Group supervision

**Most frequently used format:** Individual case consultation (in excess of 85% of responses) — rated 14th most favored by supervisors and 10th by trainees out of 17 possible rankings.

**Lowest ranked:**
- Reviewing written verbatim transcripts
- Live supervision using earphone to direct supervisee (especially low by trainees)
- Family sculpting

### Six Guidelines for Videotape Supervision (Breunlin, Karrer, McGuire, & Cimmarusti, 1988):
1. **Focus by setting supervision goals** related to trainee development
2. **Relate the internal process across contexts** so feelings may be ventilated soon after session, in context of goals
3. **Select tape segments** that focus on remedial performance or approaches trainee is capable of changing
4. **Use comments to compare observations** of the session with established goals, refining goals moderately
5. **Maintain a moderate level of arousal** — midpoint between relaxation and stress
6. Allow enough stimulation to activate supervisees, but not so much stress as to overwhelm them

### Group Supervision
- Conducted by 65% of surveyed predoctoral internship sites
- 73% of sites spending some time in group processes (Riva & Cornish, 1995)

**Benefits:**
- Economy of time, money, and expertise
- Less dependence on supervisor
- Provision for trainees' self-evaluation relative to peers
- Multiple perspectives on each client and exposure to more clients
- Modeling of giving feedback to promote growth
- Ability of supervisor to observe trainee in different context
- Facilitation of risk-taking and action techniques
- Group provides reflective space, mirror, and secure base for beginning trainee (Scanlon, 2002)
- Emotional learning enhanced by informal camaraderie (Marcus & King, 2003)

**Hindering processes** (Enyedy et al., 2003): between-member problems, problems with supervisors, supervisee anxiety/negative affects, logistical constraints, poor group time management.

**Facilitating group supervision:** enhance positive aspects of group interaction and feedback; address diversity issues affecting process; provide structure (process and time); normalize anxiety.

### Live Supervision
**Advantages:** Highest level of client protection; best vicarious learning; possibility of taking on more difficult clients; immediate feedback; access to actual trainee and client verbal/nonverbal behaviors; potential for supervision intervention and treatment intervention.

### Video Review
Same advantages as live supervision. Contrary to popular belief, supervisor observation behind one-way mirror, audio taping, and videotaping do NOT elicit significant deleterious counselor anxiety and performance (Ellis, Krengel, & Beck, 2002).

## 2.8 Mentoring

**Definition** (Johnson, 2002): Someone who "provides the protege with knowledge, advice, challenge, counsel and support in the protege's pursuit of becoming a full member of a particular profession."

**Distinguished from supervision by:** volitional quality (sought out by protege); lack of evaluative or legal component; longer duration. However, some supervisors become mentors.

**Benefits:** Mentor's charges advance more rapidly in careers; report enhanced professional-identity development and career satisfaction.

**Prevalence:** Fewer clinical psychology students (53%) mentored than other areas of psychology (73%); PsyDs less likely than PhDs to be mentored.

### Dysfunctional Mentoring Prevention (Johnson & Huwe, 2002):
- Creation of a culture of mentoring
- Assessment of new faculty hires as potential mentors
- Using mentorship skills as criterion when hiring
- Mentor training
- Mentor monitoring
- Providing rewards for faculty mentors
- Creation of clear structure for addressing dysfunction

## 2.9 Culture in Supervision

**Fukuyama (1994a) — Critical Incidents:**
Supervisees valued:
- Openness and support; not being stereotyped
- Supervisors demonstrating belief in their clinical work, especially with cultural issues
- Culturally-specific supervision addressing cultural implications
- Opportunities for multicultural experience

Negative incidents: supervisor lack of awareness of cultural-specific norms; use of expressions offensive to supervisee; questioning supervisee interpretation of client behavior as culturally relevant when supervisee and client shared same cultural heritage.

**Cross-cultural barriers** (McRoy et al., 1986): language barriers; prejudice/bigotry; lack of knowledge of cultural differences; student defensiveness; differences in opinions, background, and life experiences.

**Key finding:** 30% of predoctoral interns and 70% of supervisors had never completed a multicultural counseling course (Constantine, 1997).

**Minimum requirement** (J.M. Bernard, 1994): Being as multiculturally competent as one's supervisees.

## 2.10 Gender in Supervision

- Overall quality of supervision unrelated to supervisor's gender (Allen et al., 1986)
- Less encouragement of assumption of power in female trainees (Nelson & Holloway, 1990)
- Female relational focus vs. male client focus (Sells et al., 1997)

**Male supervisees:** Best supervisors placed heavy emphasis on evaluation and peer observation; found technical aspects more salient. Worst: had to compete with other supervisees for attention; did not teach practical skills; did not encourage exploration of new strategies.

**Female supervisees:** Best: absence of sexist attitudes and practices. Worst: used sexist language; emphasized traditional sex role stereotypes; subtly devalued on basis of sex; violated personal privacy.

**Feminist supervisors:** Described unconditional positive regard combined with intellectual and personal challenge as best. Worst: overdirecting/requiring conformity; pathologizing supervisee conflicts; promoting sexist or racist interpretations.

## 2.11 Key Findings Summary for Practice

### Hallmarks of Best Supervisors:
- Bidirectional trust, respect, and facilitation
- Committing enthusiasm and energy
- Amount of time devoted; sanctity of time commitment
- Sensitivity to developmental needs
- Encouragement of autonomy
- Disclosure of discomfort
- Comfort in disclosure of perceived errors by either member
- Supervisor takes responsibility for identifying discomfort/conflict
- Confrontation as desirable component
- Clarity of expectations, regular feedback, evaluation mechanisms
- **Critical:** Responding nondefensively to supervisees' negative feedback

### Competencies Inferred from "Best" Supervision Literature:
1. Capacity to enhance trainee self-confidence through support, appropriate autonomy, and encouragement
2. Capacity to model strong working alliances and develop strong supervisory alliances with the supervisee
3. Ability to dispense feedback, give constructive criticism, and provide formative and summative evaluation
4. Knowledge of multiple formats of supervision and skill in each of these formats
5. Adaptability and flexibility
6. Excellent communication of case conceptualization, with strong theoretical underpinnings
7. Ability to maintain equilibrium and, as appropriate, a sense of humor, even in the face of crises
8. Ability to identify and bring up potential conflict situations or areas of discomfort with the supervisee
9. Openness to self-evaluation and to evaluation by supervisees and peers

### Veach's (2001) Framework for Self-Assessment of Supervisory Problems:
Is the difficulty caused by: lack of knowledge, lack of skills, motivational issues, personal impairment, transference-countertransference issues, cultural difference, or administrative constraints?

# CHAPTER 3: BUILDING TECHNICAL COMPETENCE

## 3.1 The Competent Psychologist: History and Current Conceptions

Competence defined as "learned ability to adequately perform a task, duty or role" (Roe, 2002) involving integration of knowledge, skills, and values, plus achievement of a standard accepted by the field (Kaslow, 2002).

**Metacompetencies:** Abilities to judge one's own level of acquisition and use of competencies.

**Subcompetencies:** Functions that integrate knowledge, skills, and attitudes (e.g., administering tests, conducting group therapy).

Competence is necessary but not sufficient for performance — personal factors (motivation, energy) and situational factors (social support, management, availability of tools) also critical.

### Mission Bay Conference Resolutions (Polite & Bourg, 1991):
- **Relationship competency** (establishing/maintaining therapeutic relationship) described as foundational and prerequisite for all other competencies
- **Knowledge:** knowledge of self and others; expert knowledge of relevant data in one or more areas of psychology
- **Skills:** communicating empathy; engaging others; setting others at ease; establishing rapport; communicating respect
- **Attitudes:** intellectual curiosity; flexibility; scientific skepticism; open-mindedness; psychological health; belief that change can occur; appreciation of diversity; integrity and honesty; capacity for empathy; respect for others; personal relatedness; self-awareness

### Shaw and Dodson (1988) — Competent Psychologist Manifests:
(a) Use of a theoretical or conceptual frame to direct therapy
(b) Memory of the client's central issues
(c) Skillful use of intervention techniques to promote desired change or set the stage for conditions of change
(d) Knowledge of when to apply (or not apply) these interventions
(e) Knowledge of self and role of the self (added by Falender & Shafranske)
(f) Knowledge of the role of culture, ethnicity, gender, and variables of diversity (added)

### Six Competency Areas (Sumerall et al., 2000):
1. Development and maintenance of effective working relationships
2. Assessment
3. Intervention and use of advanced clinical skills
4. Research — systematic model of inquiry
5. Education — facilitation to enhance growth of knowledge, skills, attitudes in the learner
6. Management — activities that direct, organize, or control services, including supervision
7. Ethics — knowledge and execution of problem-solving strategies addressing conflicts related to moral values, ethics, and law

### APA Accreditation Domain B Requirements:
Interns must demonstrate intermediate to advanced level in: (a) theories and methods of assessment/diagnosis and effective intervention (including empirically supported treatments); (b) theories/methods of consultation, evaluation, and supervision; (c) strategies of scholarly inquiry; (d) issues of cultural and individual diversity relevant to all of the above.

## 3.2 Competency Assessment Approaches

### MASTERY Method (Fantuzzo, 1984):
- **M**astering knowledge
- **A**ssessing skill competency
- **S**etting minimal competency standards
- **T**raining to competency
- **E**valuating understanding of relevant legal and ethical principles
- **R**eviewing skill level
- **Y**ielding to continuing education

### Approaches from Medicine:
- Focus on physician's cognitive process: clinical reasoning, clinical judgment, problem-solving
- **Critical incidents:** candidates identify from own experience incidents that had positive or negative effect on quality of patient care (Norman, 1985)
- Medical definition of competence: "the habitual and judicious use of communication, knowledge, technical skills, clinical reasoning, emotions, values, and reflection in daily practice for the benefit of the individual and the community being served" (Epstein & Hundert, 2002)

### Approaches from Dentistry:
Competencies classified into domains: professionalism, assessment of patient and oral environment, establishment and maintenance of healthy oral environment, restoration, health promotion, practice administration. Self-assessment is intrinsic part of evaluation.

### Approaches from Business Management (McClelland, 1998):
Competencies distinguishing outstanding performers: achievement orientation, analytic thinking, conceptual thinking, flexibility, impact and influence, initiative, self-confidence.

### Psychiatry — Supervisee Attributes (Rodenhauser, Rudisill, & Painter, 1989):
1. **Basic personal qualities:** openness, reliability, integrity
2. **Facilitators of relationships with supervisors:** interest, motivation, willingness to take initiative, enthusiasm, eagerness
3. **Facilitators of relationships with patients:** interpersonal curiosity, flexibility, empathy
4. **Facilitators of content/theory learning:** intellectual openness, intellectual curiosity, capacity for conceptual abstraction
5. **Facilitators of process/skills learning:** minimal defensiveness, receptivity to feedback, tolerance for ambiguity

### Measurement Properties (Neufeld, 1985):
Face validity; comprehensiveness/content validity; reliability; concurrent, predictive, and construct validity; economic and time feasibility; match with purpose of measurement. Multiple modalities preferred over single technique.

### Outstanding vs. Mediocre Trainee Factors (Peterson & Bry, 1980):
Four common factors for outstanding: responsibility, interpersonal warmth, intellectual acuity, and qualities acquired through experience (self-assurance, technical skill, self-sufficiency, maturity, dedication).

(b) Training contract laying out component parts of required training sequence
(c) Graduate school report of competencies and areas needing improvement
(d) Supervisory plan focused on identified competencies
(e) Formal evaluation of each competency component at minimum 3-4 points during training year

## 3.4 Supervisory Goals

Development of supervision plan relating to assessed competencies, developmental level of trainee, and designated goals linked to supervisory contract.

**For remediation:** Identify specific area of deficit; center plan around specific experiences; observable goals identified with action steps.

**Example — increasing competence with preschool-age children with behavioral disorders:** coleading preschool group; assigned family with child that age and presenting problems; reading appropriate literature and treatment manuals.

**Example — identifying emotional expression:** review tapes; supervisor assist in identifying emotions unrecognized during session; action plan to identify own feeling states then those of client; articulate evidence that goal has been reached.

## 3.5 Competency of the Psychology Supervisor

### Requirements (Cobia & Boes, 2000):
- To be competent in the areas of the supervisee's practice
- To limit supervision to areas in which one has had sufficient training, supervised experience, or both
- Not to provide supervision in areas in which one is not entitled to practice

### Two Fundamental Measurable Supervision Competencies (Nelson et al., 2001):
1. The ability to establish strong alliances with supervisees
2. The ability to manage interpersonal conflicts in supervision

### APPIC Competencies Conference — Supraordinate Factors:
- Recognition that attaining supervision competencies is a lifelong, cumulative developmental process with proficiency levels beyond mere competence
- Attention to all forms of diversity
- Attention to legal and ethical issues influenced by professional and personal factors (values, beliefs, biases, conflicts)
- Necessity of peer assessment and self-assessment across all levels of development

### ACES Standards — 11 Core Characteristics of Effective Supervisors:
1. Being an effective counselor first
2. Demonstrating particular traits and characteristics
3. Knowledgeable about legal and ethical considerations
4. Knowledgeable about the supervisory relationship
5. Knowledgeable about methods and techniques of supervision
6. Knowledgeable about the developmental process
7. Knowledgeable about case conceptualization
8. Knowledgeable about assessment and evaluation
9. Knowledgeable about record keeping and report writing
10. Knowledgeable about evaluation
11. Knowledgeable about supervision research

### Self-Assessment Model (Belar et al., 2001):
Steps to gauge readiness for new forms of practice:
1. Organize a sequential set of competencies with reference to current research
2. Consult with relevant professional peers, colleagues, or other professionals
3. Identify a mentor
4. Identify a stepwise progression of experiential components
5. Create a peer-learning group around the particular area

### Self-Monitoring (Cone, 2001):
Two actions: identifying a behavior and recording its occurrence. Useful for targeting particular specific behaviors but problematic as assessment technique because it focuses on only one finite behavior.

## 3.6 Transition from Clinician to Supervisor

### Three Stances of Varying Effectiveness (Borders, 1992):

**Stance 1 — Supervisee as Surrogate for Supervisor-Clinician:**
- Supervisor performs as clinician: making copious notes about client, generating hypotheses, devising plans
- Emphasis on client to exclusion of supervisee's reactions or questions
- Supervisee feels disempowered and overwhelmed

**Stance 2 — Supervisee as Client:**
- Supervisor focuses on supervisee's personal issues
- Assumes supervisee's dynamics are sole reason for performance shortcomings
- Inadequate assessment: not differentiating what skills supervisee actually has vs. skills they may be afraid of using

**Stance 3 — Supervisee as Learner; Supervisor as Educator:**
- **Goal position** — strategizing to help supervisee be more effective with client
- Priority given to learning supervisee's needs and meeting those needs
- Proactive stance within discovery learning process
- Client welfare remains tantamount

**Higher level (authors' addition):** Supervisor as coordinator of **bidirectional process** — influences and is influenced by supervisee behavior. Similar to narrative-supervision process (Bob, 1999) — dialogue exploring constructed realities of client, supervisee, and supervisor.

### Methods to Progress as Supervisor (Borders, 1992):
- Review of session videotapes
- Advance planning of supervisory sessions
- Use of supervisory case notes to link goals to events and successful execution
- Live observation and supervision
- Interpersonal Process Recall (Kagan & Kagan, 1997)
- Peer review
- Modeling of effective supervision

## 3.7 Training Models for Supervisors

### Three Types (Getz, 1999):
1. **Skill-development programs** — enhance supervisee's conceptual and other skills through teaching; approach the task of helping the client
2. **Personal-growth model** — enhance insight, perspective, and sensitivity through focusing on supervisee's personal responses to client
3. **Integration model** — integrate skills and personal awareness in relating to client

### Supervisory Role Models:
- Teacher; Counselor; Consultant (Ellis & Dell, 1986)
- Facilitator; Administrator (J.M. Bernard & Goodyear, 1998)
- Monitor-evaluator, Instructor-advisor, Model, Supporter-sharer, Consultant (Holloway, 1999)
- Progression: Teacher → Guide → Gatekeeper → Consultant (Taibbi, 1995)

### Seven Elements of Supervisory Roles/Tasks (Carroll, 1999):
1. The relationship task
2. The teaching/learning task
3. The counseling task
4. The monitoring task
5. The evaluation task
6. The consultation task
7. The administrative task

### Combined Model (Holloway + Carroll):
1. Creating a forum of reflection (consulting)
2. Mentoring (modeling, supporting, and sharing)
3. Building an apprenticeship (monitoring, evaluating, and advising)
4. Connecting supervision to education
5. Weaving a tapestry of theory and intervention (instructing)
6. Incorporating awareness of developmental models of supervision
7. Enhancing awareness of social role models of supervision
8. Integrating diversity, context, and legal and ethical considerations

### J.M. Bernard's Structured Workshop in Supervision:
Uses meta- and experiential activities. Trainees observe videos of client therapy sessions; play role of supervisor taking notes; organize content of supervisory hour. Activities categorized by discrimination model foci (intervention, conceptualization, personalization) with supervisor acting as teacher, counselor, or consultant. Progression: observation → role-play dyads (reverse roles) → audiotaping all supervision sessions for review → six more sessions covering Discrimination Model, Interpersonal Process Recall (IPR), Microtraining, live supervision, evaluation, ethical and legal issues.

### Borders et al. (1991) Curriculum:
Seven curricular areas: (1) models; (2) supervisee development; (3) methods and techniques; (4) the supervisory relationship; (5) ethical, legal, and professional regulatory issues; (6) evaluation; (7) administrative skills. Includes self-awareness as significant strength.

### Powell et al. (1990) Core Curriculum:
Through task analysis: process issues in negotiation with supervisees (win-win paradigm), giving and receiving feedback, assessing trainee work, dealing with difficult issues (trust, boundaries, interpersonal issues, unsatisfactory work).

### Novice Supervisor Group Supervision Challenges (Ellis & Douce, 1994):
Supervisor anxiety, difficulty in intervention choice, dynamics of group cohesion (competition vs. support), responsibility (tension between client welfare and supervisee growth), parallel process, power struggles, individual differences, sexual attraction.

### Competencies for Building Technical Competence:
- Vision to establish competencies corresponding to the setting and agenda of the training
- Technical skills to define and articulate component parts of tasks and responsibilities within the training sequence
- Ability to interweave established competencies with goals and assessed skills of trainees

# CHAPTER 4: ADDRESSING PERSONAL FACTORS IN SUPERVISION

## 4.1 Values and Psychotherapy

- The assumption that psychotherapy is value neutral is no longer tenable
- Clinical theories include perspectives on human nature; some are "modern forms of religious thinking" (Browning, 1987)
- Psychotherapy is a process of behavior change involving reordering of beliefs and values within an implicit moral framework (Prilleltensky, 1997)
- Clinicians and supervisors need to consider the role of personal influence in their practices
- One aim of supervision is to assist supervisee to become mindful that psychotherapy is a value-laden enterprise

## 4.2 Countertransference

### Definition and Sources
Countertransference broadly applies to personal reactions of the psychotherapist that potentially influence treatment.

**Sources include:**
- Transference reactions (Freud, 1910)
- Projections of the client (Heimann, 1950)
- Identifications with the client and others (Racker, 1953)
- Products of the intersubjective nature of the relationship (Natterson, 1991)
- All personal beliefs and values of the therapist

**Authors' intersubjective view:** "The very formation of the therapeutic [and supervisory] process is derived from an inexplicably intertwined mixture of the clinical [and supervisory] participants' subjective reactions to one another. Knowledge of the patient's [and supervisee's] psychology is considered contextual and idiosyncratic to the particular clinical [supervisory] interaction" (Dunn, 1995).

### Enactments
Occur when both parties participate in complementary fashion, often reflecting convergence of individual psychological conflicts (Chused, 1991; Hirsch, 1998; Jacobs, 1986; Johan, 1992; McLaughlin, 1987, 1991; Roughton, 1993). By definition co-constructed and serve both individual and shared motivations. Both parties are drawn into and initiate/coconstruct enactments through their own subjective influences.

The goal is NOT elimination of countertransference (an impossible objective) but rather to bring countertransference **into the service of these professional relationships**.

### Parallel Process
Originally conceptualized as therapist unconsciously identifying with an aspect of the client and then enacting the client's dynamics with the supervisor (Arlow, 1963). From intersubjective perspective: "a parallel process can begin with the patient, supervisee, or supervisor; involve the other dyadic partner first in an intradyadic relational pattern; then, 'carried' by the analyst/supervisee, influence the second dyad to enact a related transference-countertransference matrix" (Frawley-O'Dea & Sarnat, 2001). Parallel processes hold potential to sweep all participants into counterproductive cycle of enactments, straining both therapeutic and supervisory alliances.

### Research on Countertransference
- Acting under the sway of countertransference is harmful (Gelso & Hayes, 2001)
- Hayes et al. (1997) found countertransference was not successfully managed in poor/moderate outcome cases; adverse effect proportionate to amount of countertransference exhibited
- Boundary violations and egregious acts of professional misconduct illustrate dangers of mismanagement

### Factors Essential to Countertransference Management (Gelso & Hayes, 2001):
1. Self-insight
2. Self-integration
3. Empathy
4. Conceptualizing ability
5. Ability to manage anxiety

These factors were: associated with ratings of excellence in therapists; negatively related to supervisees' displays of countertransference behaviors (Friedman & Gelso, 2000); positively associated with clinician/supervisor ratings of outcome (Gelso et al., 2002).

## 4.3 Addressing Countertransference in Supervision

### When to Initiate Inquiry
- Following supervisees' reports of being frustrated, bored, distracted, confused, or irritated
- When departures from supervisee's usual clinical conduct and disruptions of therapeutic frame have occurred
- When treatment appears to be going nowhere
- When observing therapist behaviors that appear counterproductive, inconsistent with treatment goals, or suggestive of defensiveness or disengagement

### How to Address
1. Foundation of a well-established supervisory alliance in which consideration of personal values and factors has been routinely encouraged and modeled
2. Encouraging self-reflection
3. Expanding inquiry to elucidate associations and affective/behavioral reactions through focusing techniques
4. Supervisor self-disclosure to model and encourage forthrightness; normalize the process
5. Video observation to identify particular sequences where unusual shifts in supervisee's demeanor, behavior, and affects occur
6. Assess impact of countertransference on therapeutic alliance
7. Where strain/rupture in alliance has occurred: training in metacommunication (Safran & Muran, 2000)

### Critical Boundary Maintenance
- Inquiry directs attention to interactions and processes specific to the supervised case
- Personal issues of supervisee may surface but are considered in light of the case
- Encouraging exploration of personal conflicts in supervision compromises supervisory alliance and serves neither client nor supervisee
- When countertransference significantly compromises professional development: recommendation for consultation; referral to private psychotherapy with clinician not associated with training program

## 4.4 Self-Disclosure

### Definition
"Statements that reveal something personal about therapists" (Hill & Knox, 2001, p. 413). Self-disclosures are broadly defined to include implicit conveyances through selection of content attended to, observations made, interventions offered, and manner of conducting oneself.

### Types of Self-Disclosure

#### Type 1: Intentional Self-Disclosure
**Frequency:** Approximately 3.5% of interventions across studies (Hill & Knox, 2001)

**Purposes include:**
- Increasing perceived similarity
- Modeling appropriate behavior
- Fostering the therapeutic alliance
- Normalizing clients' experiences
- Offering alternative ways of thinking and acting
- Responding to clients' requests for disclosure

**Therapeutic Practice Guidelines** (Hill & Knox, 2001):
1. Therapists should generally disclose infrequently
2. Most appropriate topic: professional background; Least appropriate: sexual practices and beliefs
3. Use disclosures to validate reality, normalize client experiences, model appropriate behavior, strengthen therapeutic alliance, or offer alternative ways to think/act
4. Avoid disclosures chiefly for own needs, that remove focus from client, interfere with session flow, burden/confuse client, are intrusive, blur boundaries, or overstimulate client
5. Therapist self-disclosure in response to client self-disclosure is particularly effective in eliciting client disclosure
6. Observe carefully how clients respond; ask about client reactions; use information to conceptualize and decide how to intervene
7. It may be especially important to disclose with clients who have difficulty forming relationships in therapeutic setting

**Supervisor self-disclosure considerations:**
- Useful to extent it builds/maintains supervisory alliance and prompts active participation in learning cycle
- Self-disclosure not supporting this process is counterproductive, gratuitous, and may reflect countertransference needs (e.g., to be admired) or inadequate supervision training

#### Type 2: Unintentional Self-Disclosure
- Reflects role of countertransference in treatment and supervisory processes
- May take form of disclosing personal facts, background, attitudes, reactions; providing opinions and advice concerning relationships and situations

**Phenomenology** — the "felt sense" of departing from appropriate practice:
- Discomfort regarding intrusion of personal influence
- Therapist/supervisor may experience confusion, anxiety, or shame following disclosure
- May be completely unaware of disclosure — becomes known only through reactions of others or review of session

**How to handle:**
- Approach such self-disclosures as **useful data**
- Avoid critical or shame-inducing feedback
- Provide opportunities to understand countertransference pressures within the relationship
- Mitigate undue influence

#### Type 3: Unexpected Exposures (B. Gerson, 1996; Gold & Nemiah, 1993; Ulman, 2001)
- Not technically disclosures because clinician neither intentionally nor unintentionally (under countertransference pressure) directly self-revealed
- Occur through: unavoidable/obvious circumstances (pregnancy, unexpected illness, miscarriage); public exposure (lectures, publications, awards); client/supervisor gaining information informally (overhearing reference in waiting room)
- Still affect therapeutic/supervisory relationship and should be processed in supervision
- Supervision helps supervisee determine what to disclose and how to address boundary setting
- Intent: neither burden client emotionally nor disengage under presumption of anonymity

### Metacommunication and Self-Disclosure
Self-disclosure as a feature of metacommunication provides a form of self-explanation establishing "an atmosphere of absolute candor" (Renik, 1995). "There are times when we need to think aloud with the patient" (Cooper, 1998). Countertransference may be revealed in the process of metacommunication.

## 4.5 Supervisor Countertransference

### Sources (Ladany, Constantine, Miller, Erikson, & Muse-Burke, 2000):
1. **Intern's personal style**
2. **Supervisor's unresolved personal issues**
3. **Interactions between intern and supervisory environment**
4. **Problematic client-intern interactions**
5. **Intern-supervisor interactions**
6. **Interactions between supervisor and supervisory environment**

Supervisors reported experiencing a range of emotions and included situations of parallel process. Commitment to self-reflection and use of peer supervision/consultation may enable supervisor to become aware of conflicts negatively affecting supervisory working alliance.

## 4.6 Three Stances Toward Supervisees (Borders, 1992)

1. **Supervisee as Surrogate for Supervisor-Clinician** — supervisor performs as clinician; emphasis on client to exclusion of supervisee's reactions; supervisee feels disempowered
2. **Supervisee as Client** — supervisor focuses on supervisee's personal issues as sole reason for performance shortcomings; inadequate assessment of skills
3. **Supervisee as Learner; Supervisor as Educator** — goal position; prioritizing learning supervisee's needs; proactive stance within discovery learning process; client welfare tantamount

**Bidirectional stance (higher level):** Supervisor as coordinator of bidirectional process — influences and is influenced by supervisee behavior; dialogue exploring constructed realities.

## 4.7 Personal Growth vs. Skill Development Models

### Three Training Program Types:
1. **Skill-development programs** — enhance supervisee's conceptual and other skills through teaching; approach the task of helping the client
2. **Personal-growth model** — enhance insight, perspective, and sensitivity through focusing on supervisee's personal responses to the client
3. **Integration model** — integrate skills and personal awareness in relating to the client

## 4.8 Complete Competencies for Addressing Personal Factors

1. **An understanding of the value-based nature of clinical and supervisory practices**
2. **An understanding of the theoretical and empirical literature concerning countertransference and its appearance in clinical and supervisory practices**
3. **Interpersonal and professional skills that contribute to the establishment and maintenance of a working supervisory alliance**, including:
   - Integrity
   - Sensitivity to diversity
   - Empathy
   - Warmth
   - Clinical competence
   - Maintenance of professional boundaries
   - Management of countertransference
4. **Supervision behaviors that foster self-insight, self-integration, anxiety management, empathy, and conceptualization abilities in the supervisee**, including:
   - Providing information and theory on countertransference
   - Observing and listening for the appearance of personal influence and countertransference
   - Attending to these occurrences in a direct, nonpunitive, and uncritical manner as phenomena to be examined
   - Engaging in open-ended inquiry
   - Providing feedback
   - Engaging in self-disclosure
   - Engaging in modeling response to in-session countertransference
5. **Monitoring the supervisory alliance and supervisor countertransference, obtaining peer supervision and consultation as appropriate**

# CHAPTER 5: ALLIANCE IN THERAPEUTIC AND SUPERVISORY RELATIONSHIPS

## 5.1 The Alliance Construct

### Empirical Foundation
Meta-analyses over 25 years have found the therapeutic relationship and alliance to be robust variables associated with treatment outcome. Horvath (2001), conducting meta-analysis of 90 independent clinical investigations, concluded: "Two decades of empirical research have consistently linked the quality of the alliance between therapist and client with therapy outcome. The magnitude of this relation appears to be independent of the type of therapy and whether outcome is assessed from the perspective of the therapist, client, or observer."

### Bordin's (1979, 1983, 1994) Pantheoretical Alliance Model

**Three interrelated features:**
1. **Change Goals** — mutually determined goals (e.g., mastering specific skills, enlarging understanding of clients, maintaining standards of service)
2. **Tasks** — the means to achieve goals; collaborative work toward achievement
3. **Bond** — partner compatibility "grows out of their experience of association in a shared activity [and] is likely to be expressed and felt in terms of liking, trusting, respect for each other, and a sense of common commitment and shared understanding in the activity"
4. **Strain** — significant deviation in patient's commitment to the working alliance

**Key insight:** The kinds of problems that brought the patient to therapy will become manifest in the treatment relationship and will become a necessary focus of therapeutic work. Strains during initial alliance formation suggest more significant difficulties in forming relationships; strains after alliance formed reflect self-defeating propensities being repeated.

**"The work of the alliance becomes the rebuilding of the damaged alliance and thus the acquisition of new ideas about self and relationships"** (Bordin, 1976, 1989).

### Supervisory Working Alliance (Bordin, 1983)
Based on establishment of mutually determined goals and means to achieve them. An emotional bond and alliance forms through process of working collaboratively toward achievement of goals. Relational qualities are seen by supervisees as important.

## 5.2 Safran & Muran's Rupture and Repair Model

### Broadened Conceptualization (Safran & Muran, 1998)
Four implications:
1. Highlights interdependence of relational and technical factors — meaning of any technical factor can be understood only in interpersonal context
2. Provides rational framework for guiding therapist's interventions in flexible fashion
3. Ruptures in the therapeutic alliance are the key to understanding the patient's representational world
4. Understanding patients as diverse in capacity and variable in experiencing highlights importance of negotiation about tasks and goals of therapy

## 5.3 Alliance Rupture Markers

### Two Major Categories of Ruptures (based on Bordin, 1979):
1. **Conflicts in tasks and goals of treatment** — disagreements, misunderstanding, confusion regarding treatment procedures; differences in beliefs/values; misattunements from culturally anchored experiences
2. **Problems in the bond dimension** — breaches of trust and empathy; intense transference-countertransference enactments; negative attributions about the other

### Two Manifestation Types:

#### Withdrawal Ruptures
Markers include:
- Disengagement from the therapist
- Disengagement from client's own emotions
- Disengagement from the therapeutic process
- **Compliance** (which is ineffectual)
- Indirect communication of negative sentiments or hostility
- Nonresponsiveness to therapist interventions

#### Confrontation Ruptures
Markers include:
- Overt expression of negative sentiments
- Disagreement about the goals or tasks of therapy
- Self-esteem enhancing operations
- Overt expressions of anger, resentment, or dissatisfaction with therapist or treatment

### Omer's (1994, 2000) Three Roads to Therapeutic Impasse:
1. Therapist and client develop a hopeless narrative about client's difficulties
2. Therapeutic strategy grinds to a halt
3. Therapeutic interaction becomes trapped in a negative pattern

## 5.4 Complete Alliance Repair Process — Four Stages

### Safran & Muran (2000b, 2000c) Stage-Process Model

#### Stage 1: Attending to the Rupture Marker
- **Withdrawal rupture:** Client displays withdrawal rupture marker (C1) → Therapist focuses client on immediate experience (T1)
- **Confrontation rupture:** Therapist begins by becoming aware of feelings evoked in them and metacommunicating about the interaction, rather than continuing to participate in the dysfunctional cognitive-interpersonal cycle
- Therapist must shift from being coparticipant in maladaptive interpersonal cycle
- Therapist actively elicits exploration and disclosure of thoughts and feelings regarding the rupture
- **Critical:** Therapist must be in a posture of neutrality (openness) and nondefensiveness
- Supervisees need to be explicitly prepared to accept perceptions differing from their own
- Purpose is NOT to assign blame but to provide empathic understanding and modify own behavior

#### Stage 2: Exploration of Rupture Experience
- Client expresses negative feelings mixed with rupture marker (C2)
- Therapist facilitates self-assertion (T2)
- Focus on exploration of thoughts and feelings associated with the rupture

#### Stage 3: Exploration of Avoidance
- Client discloses block (C3a)
- Therapist probes block (T3)
- Client explores block (C3b)
- Focus on factors or resistances restricting exploration

#### Stage 4: Self-Assertion
- Client self-asserts (C4)
- Therapist validates assertion (T4)
- Expression of negative sentiments initially includes disowning of responsibility
- Followed by deepening awareness of disavowed needs
- Finally: acceptance and communication to therapist of wishes, needs, and feelings

**Outcome:** Resolution of alliance ruptures not only maintains therapeutic relationship but also facilitates change processes directly related to relational difficulties. For clients: involves in vivo learning and corrective emotional experiences. For novice clinicians: fosters development of important clinical competencies.

### Central to Repair Process:
Close attention to the **here-and-now interpersonal dynamics**, regardless of technical differences inherent in clinical theories.

## 5.5 Metacommunication Techniques

### Definition (Safran & Muran, 2000b):
The "attempt to step outside of the relational cycle that is currently being enacted by treating it as the focus of collaborative exploration: that is, communicating about the transaction or implicit communication that is taking place" (emphasis in original).

### Connection to Interpersonal Process Recall (Kagan & Kagan, 1997):
Supervisee describes underlying thoughts and feelings that occurred during therapeutic interaction. Provides means to process dynamics that shaped clinical process and builds skills in metacognition essential to metacommunication.

### Requirements (Binder & Strupp, 1997a):
Involves "reflection in action" (Schon, 1987) — the ability to observe process as one is participating in it and to improvise effective strategies while in the midst of acting.

### Key Elements:
- Bringing the relationship to new level of collaboration where immediate interaction is focus of inquiry
- Willingness to engage in "here-and-now analysis of maladaptive cognitions, sentiments, and behaviors that are recurrently enacted in patterned form in the therapeutic relationship"
- Includes challenging clients to disclose their experiences of the therapeutic interaction
- Therapist may find self-perceptions and behaviors challenged by client's observations

### Research Support:
Kivlighan and Schmitz (1992) found improvement in poor working alliances was related to increases in counselor challenges and focus placed on the relationship.

## 5.6 How Supervisory Alliance Differs from Therapeutic Alliance

- Supervisory relationship bears unique **power differential** — supervisee cannot easily leave without threat (unlike client)
- Supervisory alliance has **evaluative component** — fears regarding negative evaluations may weaken working alliance (Burke, Goodyear, & Guzzard, 1998)
- Supervisor bears **multiple roles** (clinician, educator, evaluator) simultaneously
- Supervisor has responsibilities to the profession, training institution, and public beyond the dyad
- Therapist attributes negatively affecting formation of therapeutic alliance can equally influence supervisor-supervisee relationship — effects can be **more deleterious** due to power differential
- Same positive therapist attributes apply positively to supervisory alliance: flexible, honest, respectful, trustworthy, confident, warm, interested, open (Ackerman & Hilsenroth, 2003)
- Same positive techniques: exploration, reflection, noting past success, accurate interpretation

## 5.7 Dealing with Negative Reactions to Clients

### For Novice Clinicians at Particular Risk:
- Performance anxiety
- Inexperience
- Interpersonal discomfort in assuming new professional role
- Personal limitations derived from early/pervasive patterns of interpersonal relating may prompt defense-inspired behaviors

### Supervisor Interventions:
1. Reorienting supervisee to take a stance of inquiry — to decenter and step back from defensive mode of reacting
2. Focus attention on: (a) states of mind and attributions being stimulated within clinical interaction; (b) mental states being warded off; (c) interventions applied and their effects; (d) alternative behaviors
3. Help supervisee gain insight into negative countertransference enactments
4. Help obtain containment to address threats to therapeutic alliance
5. Assist therapist in shifting from negative countertransference to posture allowing objectivity

### When Supervisee Cannot or Will Not Address the Issue:
- Repeated threats to alliances across clients based primarily in relational component
- Requires careful assessment of supervisee's interpersonal and clinical competencies
- Assessment of suitability for psychotherapy practice

### When Alliance is Irreparably Compromised:
Supervisor may: conduct consultation with client; focus on client's experience; attempt to reestablish initial alliance between client and mental health institution; discuss client's expectations of treatment; reestablish treatment with same clinician, or transfer to different therapist, or supervisor as cotherapist.

## 5.8 Ruptures and Impasses in the Supervisory Alliance

### Negative Experiences in Supervision (Ramos-Sanchez et al., 2002):
- 21.4% of psychology interns and graduate students reported negative experience in supervision
- Associated with weaker supervisory alliances
- Impact appeared "global and long lasting, causing supervisees to question their choice of career and possibly change their career plans"

### Supervisor Countertransference Management (Ladany et al., 2000):
Supervisors managed reactions by: consulting with colleagues; participating in supervision group; disclosing aspects of experience with intern.

**Important to resolution:** identifying reactions as countertransference; seeking consultation; using disclosure.

**Counterintuitive finding:** Although supervisees believed supervisor countertransference initially weakened the relationship, they later stated it strengthened the supervision. Part of benefit: supervisor's facilitation of discussion and efforts to deepen intern's understanding of interpersonal dynamics in therapy.

## 5.9 Shame in Supervision

### Sources:
- Supervisees examining use of personal influence and intrusion of countertransference
- Discrepancy between performance and internally held standards
- Fear of not being approved of or admired by idealized supervisor (Talbot, 1995)
- Interpersonal as well as intrapersonal dynamics

### Hidden Manifestations of Shame (Talbot, 1995):
- Increases in nondisclosure
- Avoidance of discussion of certain cases
- Intellectualization
- Provision of experience-distant or insipid descriptions of clinical interactions

### Four Reactions to Shame (Hahn, 2001, drawing on Nathanson, 1992):
1. **Withdrawal**
2. **Avoidance**
3. **Attack on the self**
4. **Attack on others**

### Talbot's (1995) Six Recommendations for Addressing Shame:
1. **Be alert to the supervisee's disguised shame**
2. **Encourage the supervisee to explore how the therapy and the supervision are experienced**
3. **Demonstrate the qualities of a psychotherapist** by assisting the supervisee in uncovering personal material that affects the psychotherapy
4. **Create an environment of safety** where shame-related phenomena can be discussed with candor and curiosity
5. **Ease insecurities by modeling** the activities that supervisees seek to encourage from their clients
6. **Avoid attempts to dissuade the supervisee of an idealizing transference** toward the activities

## 5.10 Parallel Process in the Alliance Context

- Dynamics of therapeutic relationship stimulate and are reflected within supervisory relationship
- Therapist, through identification with client or projective identification, enacts client's dynamics with supervisor
- Rather than obtaining insight into transference-countertransference matrix, therapists and supervisors replicate unconsciously derived roles and object relations (Grey & Fiscalini, 1987)
- Supervisors may bring in interaction processes originating in contexts external to supervisory relationship

### Best Prevention:
- Supervisors being mindful of effects of personal and professional conduct
- Striving to be responsive to needs of supervisee
- Conveying clear understanding of objectives
- Clarifying mutual responsibilities

## 5.11 Alliance Measurement

### Working Alliance Inventory (Bahrick, 1989; Appendix B)
Provides a useful self-report measure to assess alliance factors (task, bond, goal) in the supervisory relationship. Two forms: Supervisee Form and Supervisor Form. Full item listings provided in Appendix B below.

# COMPREHENSIVE FRAMEWORK SUMMARY

## The Supervision Competency Model

### Foundational Assumptions:
1. Supervision is a **distinct professional activity** requiring specialized competencies
2. Effective supervision builds on **three pillars**: supervisory relationship, inquiry, educational praxis
3. Four **superordinate values:** integrity-in-relationship, ethical values-based practice, appreciation of diversity, science-informed practice
4. Competence is the **habitual and judicious use** of communication, knowledge, technical skills, clinical reasoning, emotions, values, and reflection
5. A **competency-based approach** provides explicit framework to initiate, develop, implement, and evaluate supervision processes and outcomes

### Core Supervisor Competency Domains:
1. **Relationship/Alliance** — establishing and maintaining working alliances; managing interpersonal conflict; metacommunication
2. **Technical Instruction** — defining competencies, component task analysis, multiple supervision methods
3. **Personal Factors** — countertransference management, self-disclosure judgment, maintaining boundaries
4. **Diversity Competence** — self-awareness, knowledge, culturally appropriate practices
5. **Evaluation** — formative and summative assessment; gatekeeper functions
6. **Ethics and Legal** — values-based practice, accountability, professional standards
7. **Self-Assessment** — supervisor reflexivity, peer consultation, ongoing development

### The Experiential Learning Cycle:
Experiencing → Reflecting → Conceptualizing → Planning → Experimenting

### Alliance Repair Process (Safran & Muran):
Stage 1: Attending to the Rupture Marker → Stage 2: Exploration of Rupture Experience → Stage 3: Exploration of Avoidance → Stage 4: Self-Assertion

### Key Diagnostic Tool (Veach's Framework):
When problems arise, assess whether due to: lack of knowledge, lack of skills, motivational issues, personal impairment, transference-countertransference issues, cultural difference, or administrative constraints.
