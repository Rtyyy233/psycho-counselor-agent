## CHAPTER 6: BUILDING DIVERSITY COMPETENCE IN SUPERVISION

### 1. Complete Definition of Diversity Competency in Supervision

Diversity is one of the most neglected areas in supervision training and research. The majority of attention to diversity has been devoted to culture — just one particular aspect — rather than to the broader construct. Diversity includes culture in all its aspects, as well as socioeconomic status, race, religion, disabilities or ableness, age, gender, and sexual orientation, all of which may converge and intersect (Bingham, Porche-Burke, James, Sue, & Vasquez, 2002).

**APA Ethical Standard 2.01(b) (Boundaries of Competence):** "Where scientific or professional knowledge in the discipline of psychology establishes that an understanding of factors associated with age, gender, gender identity, race, ethnicity, culture, national origin, religion, sexual orientation, disability, language, or socioeconomic status is essential for effective implementation of their services or research, psychologists have or obtain the training, experience, consultation, or supervision necessary to ensure the competence of their services, or they make appropriate referrals."

**APA "Domain D: Cultural and Individual Differences and Diversity" (Committee on Accreditation):**
- The program recognizes the importance of cultural and individual differences and diversity in the training of psychologists.
- The program has made systematic, coherent, and long-term efforts to attract and retain interns and staff from differing ethnic, racial, and personal backgrounds.
- The program acts to ensure a supportive and encouraging learning environment appropriate for the training of diverse individuals.
- The program avoids any action that would restrict program access on grounds that are irrelevant to success in internship training or a career in professional psychology.
- The program has a thoughtful and coherent plan to provide interns with relevant knowledge and experiences about the role of cultural and individual diversity in psychological phenomena and professional practice.

**Ridley, Mendoza, and Kanitz (1994):** "Educators with the best of intentions find themselves caught between the press to provide MCT (multicultural training) and the dual disadvantage of their own inadequate training and the embryonic state of the field."

13. **Spirituality and religion** — highlighted for direct attention

### 3. Multicultural Competence Definitions

**D.W. Sue, Arredondo, and McDavis (1992) — APA Division 17 Position Paper:**
Multicultural competency = "therapist's awareness of assumptions about human behavior, values, biases, preconceived notions, personal limitations; understanding the worldview of the culturally different client without negative judgments; and developing and practicing appropriate, relevant, and sensitive intervention strategies and skills in working with culturally different clients."

**Three categories:** attitudes and beliefs, knowledge, and skills.

**D.W. Sue (2001) — Redefinition adding social justice:**
"The ability to engage in actions or create conditions that maximize the optimal development of client and client systems. Multicultural counseling competence is defined as the counselor's acquisition of awareness, knowledge, and skills needed to function effectively in a pluralistic democratic society (ability to communicate, interact, negotiate, and intervene on behalf of clients from diverse backgrounds), and on an organizational/societal level, advocating effectively to develop new theories, practices, policies, and organizational structures that are more responsive to all groups."

**Ponterotto and Casas (1987) — Training-setting definition:**
"Multicultural competence includes knowledge of clients' culture and status, actual experiences with these clients, and the ability to devise innovative strategies vis-a-vis the unique client's needs. A multiculturally competent program instills in its students these competencies, infuses minority issues into all program courses ..., and has adequate representation of minority students and faculty members."

### 4. Our Definition of Diversity Competence (Falender & Shafranske)

Diversity competency includes incorporation of self-awareness by both supervisor and supervisee and is an interactive process of the client or family, supervisee-therapist, and supervisor, using all of their diversity factors. It entails:
- Awareness, knowledge, and appreciation of the interaction among the client's, supervisee-therapist's, and supervisor's assumptions, values, biases, expectations, and worldviews
- Integration and practice of appropriate, relevant, and sensitive assessment and intervention strategies and skills
- Consideration of the larger milieu of history, society, and sociopolitical variables

### 5. Constantine and Ladany (2001) — Six Dimensions of Multicultural Competency

1. **Self-awareness:** understanding one's own multiple cultural identities, personal biases, and how socialization affects values and attitudes
2. **General knowledge of multicultural issues:** general knowledge of psychological and social issues, prejudicial attitudes, discrimination, and knowledge of emics
3. **Multicultural-counseling self-efficacy:** confidence in one's ability to perform successfully, based on a behavior set — not simply a self-perception of competence
4. **Understanding of unique client variables:** understanding how personal attributes, situations, and other factors affect client behavior
5. **Formation of an effective counseling working alliance:** includes addressing multicultural issues within the working alliance
6. **Multicultural-counseling skills:** the ability to approach multicultural issues effectively in therapy

### 6. Hansen et al. (2000) — 12 Minimal Multicultural Competencies for Practice

**Awareness:**
- Awareness of how one's own cultural heritage, gender, class, ethnic-racial identity, sexual orientation, disability, and age cohort help shape personal values, assumptions, and biases related to identified groups

**Knowledge of the following factors:**
- Historical and cultural embeddedness and change of psychological theory, inquiry methods, and professional practices
- History, manifestation, and psychological sequelae of oppression, prejudice, and discrimination
- Sociopolitical influences (e.g., poverty, stereotyping, stigmatization, and marginalization) impinging on identified groups
- Culture-specific diagnosis; normative values about illness, worldview, family structures, and gender roles; impacts on personality formation; developmental outcomes; and manifestation of illness
- Culture-specific assessment techniques

**Ability to do the following:**
- Evaluate emic and etic hypotheses
- Self-assess multicultural competence
- Modify assessment tools and qualify conclusions
- Design and implement nonbiased effective treatment plans and interventions for multiple groups

### 7. Ancis and Ladany (2001) — Domains of Multicultural Supervision Competencies

- Supervisor-focused personal development
- Supervisee-focused development
- Skills and interventions
- Process
- Outcome evaluation

**Particular competencies:**
- Self-awareness and knowledge of other cultures and worldviews
- Facilitation of supervisee diversity identity development
- Understanding of racism and oppression as well as indigenous resources
- Creation of a climate that generally facilitates diversity discussion and consideration

### 8. Acculturation Frameworks

**Brislin (2000), derived from Berry (1990):** Acculturation is whether:
(a) the family retains selected aspects of its first culture
(b) the family pursues relations with members of the donor or host culture

Four outcomes:
- **Integration:** both (a) and (b)
- **Assimilation:** only (b)
- **Separation:** only (a)
- **Marginalization:** neither (a) nor (b)

**Sodowsky, Kwan, and Pannu (1995):** Acculturation as bidirectional process — degree of adoption of "Whiteness" and retention of one's other ethnic identity.

**Trimble (2003):** "Situational acculturation" — different acculturation responses depending on the situational context.

### 9. Emic vs. Etic Parameters

- **Emic:** conceptions common to a particular ethnic or minority group, explicative
- **Etic:** conceptions universal to people across cultures

**Emic examples:**
- Personalismo: having a personal relationship with an individual (important in some Latino cultures)
- Familialism: emphasis on family over the individual (Santisteban & Mitrani, 2003)
- Nonverbal communication: eye contact, interpersonal distance, turn exchange
- Time orientation: past, present, or future focus
- Disclosure depth expectations
- "I" emphasis in Eurocentric therapies may be problematic for certain groups

**For African American clients:** Show genuine respect for complaints of racism, discrimination, and underlying cultural mistrust; avoid negative bias in diagnosis.

**For Asian Americans:** Public verbal expression of feelings can be culturally discordant; credibility of therapist was the best (and only) predictor of intent to use therapy.

**Environmental cues (Takushi & Uomoto, 2001):** "Cultural competence" of setting includes pictures on walls, ethnicities and languages in magazines, etc.

**Emic factors in supervisor-supervisee interaction (Brown & Landrum-Brown, 1995):**
- Value placed on verbal communication
- Ways language systems are similar or different
- Response to power differential and social/economic status imbalances
- Cognitive orientation (patterns of thinking and problem solving)
- Motivational orientation (role of rewards, locus of control)
- Value orientation (family vs. group, hierarchical vs. egalitarian)

### 10. How Diversity Affects the Supervisory Relationship, Case Conceptualization, and Intervention Selection

- Race can profoundly influence supervision, including trainee expectations for supervisor empathy, respect, and congruence (Leong & Wagner, 1994)
- Race can influence the supervisee's perception of whether the supervisor likes him or her
- Under some circumstances, race does not appear to influence supervision
- Multicultural supervision is defined as "a supervisor-supervisee relationship in which there are cultural differences based on race and ethnicity" (Fukuyama, 1994a)
- The institutional setting and administration are operative factors (Peterson, 1991)
- Must consider client, supervisee, and supervisor's interactive diversity variables

**Fischer, Jome, and Atkinson (1998) — Common factors contributing to client healing:**
- The therapeutic relationship
- A shared worldview of client and therapist
- Meeting client's expectations
- Use of rituals or interventions that both therapist and client view as appropriate

**For empirically supported treatments:** Decontextualize to determine cultural basis, then recontextualize according to relevant cultural features (Quintana & Atkinson, 2002).

**Research finding:** "The presence of multicultural competence is synonymous with general counseling competence" (Coleman, 1998). Attempts to present as culturally neutral resulted in clients' perceptions of lower general-counseling competence. Approximately 60% shared variance between general-counseling competence and multicultural-counseling competence (Constantine, 2002).

### 11. Specific Diversity Competence Development Models

#### A. Carney and Kahn (1984) — Five Stages of Supervisee Development of Multicultural-Counseling Competence

**Stage 1:** Little knowledge of multicultural counseling; operates on ethnocentric assumptions. Supervisor uses structured, supportive approaches; trainee self-exploration; exploration of how client has been affected by cultural/ethnic/racial group membership. Confrontation is avoided.

**Stage 2:** Increased awareness of ethnocentric attitudes/behaviors but limited understanding ("halo of naivete") — trainee may become overconfident. Supervisor continues supportive/structured environment; builds knowledge about barriers to intercultural communication; examines ethnocentric belief structures and differing client worldviews. Dissonance may propel toward Stage 3 or cause resistance.

**Stage 3:** Conflicting emotions about working with culturally different clients; wants to be respectful but limited by biases, value conflicts, and previous training. May downplay importance of race/culture. Supervisor supports frustration, acknowledges dissonance, encourages new cultural knowledge/skills. Emphasize transformation of prejudice to respect through education and exposure.

**Stage 4:** Internalizes new professional identity as competent multicultural therapist; validates worldview of others; incorporating own ethnic-racial identity with understanding of client's. Supervisor assists in understanding impact on clients, integrating disparate components of culture. Supervisees should take lead in discussing rationale for culturally competent interventions.

**Stage 5:** Advocates for rights of individuals from diverse groups; promotes and protects cultural pluralism and social equality. Supervisor moves to consultant role; assists in clarifying personal commitment, action strategies, and how to be effective agent of change.

#### B. Priest (1994) — Stages for Achieving Effective Multicultural Supervision Competency

**Stage 1:** Supervisor's denial of cultural differences that affect supervision.
**Stage 2:** Recognition that differences exist, but lack of competence about what to do — possibly feeling overwhelmed.
**Stage 3:** Attempt to identify similarities and differences among cultures that affect the supervisory relationship.
**Stage 4:** Supervisor determines where he or she fits in the cultural framework.
**Stage 5:** Beginning appreciation of cultural distinctiveness, resulting in enhanced supervisory process.
**Stage 6:** Ability to formulate methodologies respectful of supervisee's culture and interaction while acquiring new skills.

#### C. Sabnani et al. (1991) — White Racial-Identity Development Model

**Stage 1: Preexposure/Precontact** — Requires cultural emic knowledge acquisition and beginning etic counseling skills.

**Stage 2: Conflict** — Confronts dilemma of conforming to White norms while upholding humanitarian values. Requires greater understanding of prejudice/racism and emotional impact; definition of barriers; development of culturally appropriate counseling techniques.

**Stage 3: Prominority/Antiracism** — Paternal attitudes of Whites scrutinized; requires cultural immersion and study. Role-playing and communication skills training.

**Stage 4: Retreat into White Culture** — Fear or anger from Stage 3; emphasis on etic over emic approaches.

**Stage 5: Redefinition and Integration** — Integration of "Whiteness" as part of identity; enhanced respect for varying worldviews; deepening of culturally emic approaches.

#### D. Atkinson, Morten, and Sue (1993) — Minority Identity Development Model

**Conformity Stage:** Prefer values/norms of dominant group; self-deprecating attitudes about own ethnic-racial group; choose White colleagues.

**Resistance/Immersion Stage:** Increase in ethnic pride and positivity combined with increased suspicion of White Americans.

**Synergistic Stage:** Self-fulfillment associated with own ethnic/cultural/racial identity but not complete acceptance of all aspects; may become activist against oppression/discrimination.

#### E. Helms (1990) — Racial-Identity Development as Cognitive Development Continuum

**Phase 1:** Relatively less complex strategies and lower levels of racial-identity development. Elements: ethnocentrism, conformity.

**Phase 2:** More complex strategies and higher levels of racial-identity development. Elements: resistance, pseudoindependence, immersion, autonomy.

#### F. Ancis and Ladany (2001) — Model of Nonoppressive Interpersonal Development

Identifies individuals as belonging to socially oppressed group or socially privileged group (some belong to both based on different variables).

**Level 1 — Adaptation:** Conformity, complacency, apathy; superficial understanding of cultural difference; stereotyping; limited emotional awareness; denial and resistance. Supervisors minimize trainee expression of multicultural competence, use inaccurate stereotypes, perceive themselves as multiculturally competent.

**Level 2 — Incongruence:** Previously held beliefs inconsistent with experience; minimization and rationalization as defenses; remnants of stereotyping. Precipitated by major event where oppression cannot be overlooked.

**Level 3 — Exploration:** Anger about recognition of oppression; guilt and shame; increased insight. Warning: zeal of this stage may result in overemphasis on cultural factors. Supervisor-supervisee fit problem: if supervisor raises multicultural issues, supervisee at adaptation/incongruence stage will resist.

**Level 4 — Integration:** Proficiency and insight; multicultural formulations integrated into conceptualizations. Trainees use accurate empathy, analyze own biases, separate countertransference from transference.

**Most effective supervisor-supervisee combination:** Both at exploration or integration phase. Next best: trainee at integration level, more advanced than supervisor at adaptation level. This may be the most frequent constellation at present.

### 12. How to Address Cultural Ruptures or Misunderstandings in Supervision

- Directly address ruptures to the working alliance
- Consider intrapersonal and interpersonal forces affecting the relationship
- Use metacommunication — discussing the discussion itself
- Initiate discussion of race and other diversity issues (a supervisory competence)
- Process feelings at the moment they occur
- Use video review to deal with assumptions and feelings elicited during sessions
- Determine what prompted particular interventions
- Integrate affect, genuineness, and openness into discussions of diversity (Garrett et al., 2001)
- Model and describe examples of what GLB and multiculturally competent therapists say/do — and what they tend not to say/do (Phillips, 2000)
- Awareness of impact of language and assumptions (e.g., use "partner" rather than "boyfriend" to avoid assumptions of sexual orientation)
- Cook (1994): Encourage routine discussion of race of ALL participants (supervisee, supervisor, client) in supervision
- When supervisor-supervisee fit is problematic (supervisor in exploration phase, supervisee in adaptation/incongruence): expect resistance and address it directly

### 13. The "Deconstruction of the Inescapable Framework" Concept

The chapter discusses breaking down empirically supported treatments to their cultural basis and then rebuilding them with cultural sensitivity:

Quintana and Atkinson (2002): "For empirically supported treatments to be informed by multicultural thought, the treatments need to be decontextualized, to determine the cultural basis for the intervention, and then recontextualized according to relevant cultural features of the particular group." One must be well versed in emic understanding to engage in this process.

The chapter also addresses deconstruction of assumptions:
- "White people do not have a culture" — this is incorrect. Whites are as diverse as any other population, and greater consciousness needs to be raised among Whites that they do have a culture, a history, and a story.
- "Cultural provincialism is not a disease that afflicts only Caucasians" (Myers, Echemendia, & Trimble, 1991)
- The focus on particular groups as unitary (Black, White, Hispanic, gay, lesbian) has resulted in increased stereotyping and less appreciation of within-group differences. "Within-group differences have been shown to exceed between-group differences" (Suzuki, McRae, & Short, 2001)

### 14. Cultural Self-Awareness Exercises for Supervisors

**Therapist/Supervisor Maps (Wisnia & Falender, 1999):**
Construct a prioritized listing of considerations on the mind of the supervisor when going into the supervisory hour. Then construct a therapist (supervisee) map. Compare maps and discuss discrepancies and how they affect the supervisory relationship, therapeutic relationship, and outcome.

**Figure 6.1 — Supervisor Map Components:**
- Safety of Client, Family and Therapist
- Legal and Ethical Considerations
- Cultural Overlay: Emic Considerations
- Theoretical Orientation
- Attention to Process Versus Content
- View of Therapeutic Process
- Role of Therapist
- Attention to Past, Present, Integration of Two
- Articulation of Treatment Goals
- Transference-Countertransference Considerations
- Responsiveness to Client (Cues, Affect, Nonverbal Communication, Process, Cultural Variables, Respect and Perspective)
- Consistent Respect for Boundaries
- Focus on Strengths
- Interaction Between Supervisee and Supervisor (Open, Respectful, Access to and Responsiveness to Therapeutic Data, Responsiveness to Supervisory Input, Cultural Respect)
- Looking Ahead (Integration of current data into plan for future intervention, Ability to foresee possible consequences)
- Look for Matches and Mismatches

**Cultural Self-Presentation Exercise (Wisnia & Falender, 1999):**
Supervisors and supervisees describe an aspect of their own particular upbringing and personal cultural identification through one of Falicov's contexts: journey of migration and culture change, ecological context, family organization, or transitions in the structure of family life cycles. Presenters use food, music, poetry, photographic albums, video, or objects of significance. Group processes reactions, feelings elicited, and forges connectedness.

**Hays (2001) ADDRESSING Self-Assessment:**
Therapist lists own: Age/generational influences, Developmental/acquired Disabilities, Religion/spiritual orientation, Ethnicity, Socioeconomic status, Sexual orientation, Indigenous heritage, National origin, Gender. Then considers role of privilege and culture on clinical work, role of values. Then same process for client's culture.

**Zimmerman and Haddock (2001) — "In-the-box/Out-of-the-box" Exercise:**
Boxes labeled male/female. Trainees brainstorm traits associated with each gender by society. Then describe consequences of out-of-box behaviors. Also consider "common-box" shared behaviors.

**Movie viewing and discussion** in Falicov or Hays framework — movies involving cultural passage or assimilation.

**Labeling exercise** (attaching stereotypic labels to individuals' backs), making implicit associations to words associated with race, pairing culturally different partners to share cultural information (Abreu, 2001).

### 15. How to Integrate Diversity into Every Aspect of Supervision (Not as Add-on)

- **Define multicultural supervision** as relationship with cultural differences based on race/ethnicity plus institutional setting and administration (Fukuyama, 1994a; Peterson, 1991)
- Include the client/family, therapist-supervisee, and supervisor's interactive diversity variables
- Assess program climate using D'Andrea & Daniels (1991) stages: Culturally Entrenched → Cross-Cultural Awakening → Cultural Integrity → **Infusion** (multiculturalism integrated into whole curriculum)
- Lefley (1986): Training can be **additive** (new concepts added), **substitutive** (new concepts replace), or **integrative** (new concepts integral to mental health training). The goal is integrative.
- Pope-Davis, Liu, Toporek, and Brittan-Powell (2001): Requires "complete contextual change" encompassing commitment to multicultural issues AND creation/maintenance of positive environment
- Bidell, Turner, and Casas (2002): Insertion of ethnic-racial and GLB content in application materials associated with obtaining higher numbers of students with those identities
- Simply training supervisees to raise the issue of race and diversity early in supervision is a preliminary step (Hird et al., 2001)
- Full incorporation is the responsibility of the supervisor (Constantine, 1997)
- Use the Multicultural Competency Checklist (Appendix E): Minority representation (30% benchmark), curricular issues, counseling practice/supervision, research, student/faculty competency evaluation, physical environment (Ponterotto, Alexander, & Grieger, 1995)

### 16. Power Differential and Its Interaction with Diversity

- Any misuse of the power differential in the relationship or deviation from legitimate uses of authority (ultimately vested in power to evaluate) forecloses effective training and case management
- Feminist supervision theory: Those in power create meaning; reducing power causes context to change
- In feminist supervision, the supervisee is empowered; contracting increases shared responsibility (Zimmerman & Haddock, 2001)
- The "I" emphasis in Eurocentric therapies may be problematic — power dynamics differ across cultures
- Supervisor-supervisee power differential interacts with diversity when supervisor is from dominant culture and supervisee from minority culture (or vice versa)
- Cook (1994): Pairing supervisors and supervisees with differing levels of racial-identity attitudes may lead to power differentials resulting in lesser or greater expression and competency in "racial acknowledgement"

### 17. How to Discuss Diversity Openly in Supervision

- Initiate discussion of diversity factors in supervision (a specific supervisory competency)
- Use "cultural lens" to approach culture with respectful curiosity
- Discuss differences, preconceptions, and interactions among client/family, supervisee, and supervisor
- Use video review to deal with assumptions and feelings elicited
- Address supervisor's or supervisee's feelings at the moment they occur
- Determine what prompted particular interventions
- Model openness, respect, and curiosity toward all aspects of diversity
- Use "therapist maps" and "supervisor maps" to compare and discuss discrepancies
- Apply Falicov's framework to clinical material, discussing how maps of supervisor, supervisee, and client/family interface and influence the process
- Discuss myths, misconceptions, or stereotypes as awareness of similarity of experience emerges
- Train supervisees to raise issue of race and diversity early (Hird et al., 2001)
- Research finding: Only 12.5-37.9% of supervision actually discusses ethnicity, gender, and sexual orientation (Gatmon et al., 2001). Higher supervisory satisfaction associated with these discussions.

### 18. Common Diversity-Related Supervision Mistakes

1. **Not meeting minimum requirement of knowing at least as much as trainees know** about cultural competency (J.M. Bernard, 1994)
2. **Relegating culture to a single course** rather than taking an integrated approach
3. **Focusing on particular groups as unitary** (Black, White, Hispanic, gay, lesbian) — leads to stereotyping
4. **Ignoring within-group differences** — these exceed between-group differences
5. **Assuming White people don't have a culture** — failing to explore one's own cultural identity
6. **Avoiding racial issues** — White supervisors with less multicultural competence reinforce avoidance in White trainees
7. **Minimizing importance of race/salience** — race not viewed as salient to White identity
8. **Supervisees' fear of discussing diversity:** Fear of being perceived as making excuses, using Blackness as defense, being seen as pathologically preoccupied with color/discrimination
9. **"Colourless zone" mentality** in training settings (Kleintjes & Swartz, 1996)
10. **Assumption that GLB trainee will face no issues** working with GLB population
11. **Assuming heterosexual orientation** — "compulsory heterosexuality" as only acceptable orientation
12. **Biases in therapy:** Pathologizing in assessment, focusing on sexual orientation when irrelevant, lacking understanding of identity development, minimizing same-sex relationships, relying on client for education
13. **Focusing on only one identity factor** (e.g., race) when client has multiple identities
14. **Inadequate evaluation** due to failing to understand disabilities
15. **Not addressing transgender issues** — historically ignored or pathologized (Carroll & Gilroy, 2002)
16. **Supervisors overestimating their cultural discussion** — 93% of supervisors claimed they admitted lack of cross-racial experience; only 50% of supervisees acknowledged being told (Duan & Roehlke, 2001)
17. **Supervisees sensing supervisors liked/valued/respected them less** than supervisors reported

### 19. How to Enhance Diversity Competence

- **Supervised training cases** with individuals from particular cultural groups associated with higher self-competence ratings (Allison et al., 1996)
- **Personal experience combined with educational/training diversity experience** = higher competency scores (Ponterotto, Fuertes, & Chen, 2000)
- **Multicultural coursework, workshops, and supervision** cumulatively contribute to greater knowledge and skills (Pope-Davis et al., 1994)
- **Panels, guest speakers, presentations, videotapes, class discussion** — most influential parts of training (Neville et al., 1996)
- **Opportunity to interact with racially/ethnically different individuals**
- **Extensive multicultural training** associated with more differentiated ratings of culturally sensitive vs. insensitive sessions and more receptivity to introduction of race
- **Increased intensive and personal understanding** of individuals' cultural journeys and spiritual issues (Polanski, 2003)
- **Knowledge of and self-respect for one's own cultural background** constitutes core requirement

### 20. Gender and Sexual Orientation in Training

**Bruss et al. (1997) — Three-component approach:**
1. Didactic learning
2. Encouragement of trainee independence in grappling with value structure and possible homophobia
3. Learning to use "self-as-an-instrument"

**Porter (1985):** Exploration of internalized misogynist attitudes — the most difficult part of feminist supervision.

**Wester and Vogel (2002) — Male socialization consequences:**
- Focus on independence and self-reliance
- Restriction of emotional expressivity
- Toughness and aggression as coping styles
- Triggering of countertransference
- Interference with discussion of transference/countertransference

**Intervention for male trainees:** Foster historical understanding, forge understanding of socialization, encourage challenging problematic aspects while balancing encouragement of successful aspects.

**Mohr (2002) — Supervisory questions for heterosexual supervisees:**
- Address dominant working models both supervisor and supervisee hold regarding sexual orientation
- How these models might lead to errors or misconceptions

**Working models of sexual orientation:**
1. **"Essentially the same" model:** Differences only in objects of sexual attraction and lifestyle — vulnerable to stereotyping, ignores privilege differences
2. **Compulsory heterosexuality:** Heterosexuality only acceptable orientation — leads to stigmatization of GLB individuals

**Note:** Even low levels of homophobia were associated with counselor avoidance of the topic of sexual orientation (Mohr, 2002).

### 21. Training Issues in Disabilities

**Americans With Disabilities Act of 1990 definition:**
- Having a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities
- Having a record of such impairment
- Being regarded as having such impairment

**Key facts:**
- Less than 2% of APA membership identifies as individuals with disabilities (Olkin, 2002)
- Approximately 15% of U.S. population is disabled — the largest minority group
- Graduate courses on disability DECREASED from 24% (1989) to 11% (1999)
- Focus was on cognitive impairment, not physical, sensory, or psychiatric disabilities
- "Most able-bodied therapists are doing cross-cultural counseling with clients with disabilities without requisite training" (Olkin, 2002)
- Inadequate evaluations produced by professionals who fail to understand disabilities (Goodman-Delahunty, 2000)
- Specific prior training in disabilities results in more positive attitudes and prioritizing extraneous variables in treatment (Kemp & Mallinckrodt, 1996)

### 22. Age as a Diversity Factor

- By 2020, more than 20% of U.S. population will be over age 65
- Small numbers of APA members work with geriatric patients; little formal training (Qualls et al., 2002)
- **Molinari et al. (2003) — Seven competency areas for work with aging populations:**
  1. Normal aging
  2. Specialized assessment
  3. Diagnostic and treatment considerations
  4. Communication and interdisciplinary work
  5-7. Special ethical concerns
- Significant constellations of cognitive/physiological changes and negative life events unique to the population require specialized training

### 25. Specific Training Sequence for Enhancing Cultural Competency (Wisnia & Falender, 1999)

1. Movies involving cultural passage or assimilation viewed and discussed in Falicov or Hays framework
2. Construction of supervisory and therapist (supervisee) maps
3. Supervisors and supervisees describe aspect of own upbringing and cultural identification through one of Falicov's four contexts
4. Presenters use food, music, poetry, photographic albums, video, or objects of significance
5. Group processing: reactions, feelings elicited, forging of connectedness
6. Framework applied to clinical material — discussion of how maps of supervisor, supervisee, and client/family interface
7. Myths, misconceptions, stereotypes discussed
8. Personal/experiential understandings of prejudice and stereotyping processed
9. Elaborate case analysis using Falicov model and self-knowledge gleaned during seminar

### 26. Competencies for Diversity in Supervision (Summary)

- Possesses a working knowledge of the factors that affect worldview (e.g., optimism and pessimism, the value of tradition, and relationship with nature)
- Possesses self-identity awareness and competence with respect to diversity in the context of self, supervisee, and client or family
- Exhibits competence in multimodal assessment of the multicultural competence of trainees, including self-ratings, observational ratings, and supervisor and client ratings
- Models diversity and multicultural conceptualizations throughout the supervision process
- Models respect, openness, and curiosity toward all aspects of diversity and its impact on behavior, interaction, and the therapy and supervision processes
- Initiates discussion of diversity factors in supervision

## CHAPTER 7: ETHICAL AND LEGAL PERSPECTIVES AND RISK MANAGEMENT

### 1. ALL Ethical Principles Applied to Supervision

**APA Core Ethical Principles (2002 revision):**
1. **Beneficence and nonmaleficence** — do good and do no harm
2. **Fidelity and responsibility** — faithful to professional obligations
3. **Integrity** — honesty, truthfulness, accuracy
4. **Justice** — fairness, equal access
5. **Respect for people's rights and dignity** — including privacy, confidentiality, self-determination

**Koocher and Keith-Spiegel (1998) — Additional principles:**
6. According dignity — viewing others as worthy of respect
7. Treating others with caring and compassion — being considerate within professional boundaries
8. Pursuing excellence — maintaining competence and being considerate
9. Accepting accountability — considering possible consequences, accepting responsibility for action or inaction, acting with integrity

**Meara, Schmidt, and Day (1996):**
10. Veracity or truthfulness

**Haas and Malouf (1989) — General rule:**
"Make the fulfillment of personal needs subordinate to the needs of the client."

**ACES Hierarchy of priorities in decision-making (Association for Counselor Education and Supervision, 1995):**
1. Client welfare
2. Supervisee welfare
3. Supervisor welfare
4. Program or agency service and administrative needs

### 2. Due Process in Supervision (How to Handle Supervisee Problems Fairly)

Due process ensures the supervisee's rights are not violated or ignored in supervision — critical in all aspects of contracting and evaluation.

**Requirements:**
- Supervisee must have clear understanding of requirements of internship/placement
- Specific knowledge of evaluation tools and means of assessing progress
- Definition of what signifies successful completion and consequences if criteria not satisfied
- Clear operationalization of procedures presented to supervisee
- "Supervisors are responsible for informing their supervisees about their roles, expectations, goals, and criteria for evaluation at the beginning of supervision" (Cormier & Bernard, 1982)
- In a due-process case, trainee must be given: proper notice, opportunity for hearing, defense, and appeal
- Mechanisms for due process appeal of individual supervisor actions should be established and made available to all supervisees (ACES Ethical Guidelines, 2.14)

**ACES Ethical Guidelines 2.14:**
"Supervisors should incorporate the principles of informed consent and participation; clarity of requirements, expectations, roles and rules, and due process and appeal into the establishment of policies and procedures of their institution, program, courses, and individual supervisory relationships."

### 3. Informed Consent for Supervision

**Falvey (2002) — Five Levels of Informed Consent:**
1. Client consents to treatment by supervisee AND that supervisor will supervise the case
2. Supervisor and supervisee consent to supervisory responsibility and relationship
3. Institution consents to comply with clinical, ethical, and legal dimensions of supervision
4. Client must be informed that therapist is a trainee under supervision of a named individual (provision of license and contact information may be required)
5. Client must consent that confidential information and therapy process will be shared with supervisor

**Failure to obtain informed consent of client = form of malpractice (Falvey, 2002).**
**Failure to obtain consent for sharing information = "negligent breach of confidentiality" (Disney & Stephens, 1994).**

**McCarthy et al. (1995) — Contents of Professional Disclosure Statement for Supervisory Relationship:**

Supervisor background information disclosure:
- Licensure
- Academic, clinical, and supervision training information
- Professional associations
- Theoretical orientation(s) for therapy and for supervision

Contract contents:
- Logistics: frequency/duration, location, cancellation procedures
- Learning contract for successful completion
- Objectives of supervision process
- Expectations of contributions of each objective
- Advance preparation and presentation materials
- Requirements for audio or videotaping

Evaluation and due-process considerations:
- Type, expectations, criteria, specific format, frequency of administration
- Performance competencies for satisfactory employment/completion of training
- Preferably in written format, signed by supervisee and supervisor
- Adherence to legal and ethical standards clearly stated and agreed to
- Due-process procedures covered (in contract or site handbook)

**Confidentiality limits must be articulated:**
- What information supervisor may share among training staff, administrative supervisors, graduate faculty
- Information gleaned in supervision will be used for evaluation of clinical skills, supervisee competency (Patrick, 1989), and supervisory team program planning

### 4. Confidentiality Limits in Supervision

**Key principles:**
- "The law of no surprises" and "the parsimony principle" (Behnke, Preis, & Bates, 1998) — address limits of confidentiality relevant to particular client; when disclosure necessary, disclose only what is necessary and sufficient
- Students/interns/unlicensed postdoctoral fellows/supervisees are NOT specifically covered by privilege statutes (Koocher & Keith-Spiegel, 1998)
- In some circumstances, trainees may be covered by privilege accorded to communication with licensed supervisor — but state laws vary widely
- Supervisor must be aware of status of supervisees under state law

**Additional confidentiality dimensions:**
- On-line supervision presents unique challenges (loss of objective control over transmitted data) (Kanz, 2001)
- HIPAA privacy rule compliance required for health-care providers transmitting protected health information electronically
- "Domestic violations of confidentiality" (Woody, 1999) — accidental disclosures to significant others or in day-to-day life
- Accidental encounters with clients (Pulakos, 1994; Sharkin & Birky, 1992)

**Jaffee v. Redmond (1996):** U.S. Supreme Court ruled that communications between psychotherapist and patient are privileged in federal courts.

**Child/adolescent clients:** Supervisee must tailor developmentally appropriate level of involvement in consent; communicate meaningfully with comprehensible language; clarity on limits of confidentiality and what will be disclosed to parents. Gustafson and McNamara (1999): Process includes pretreatment family meetings, explanations of rationale for confidentiality decisions, written professional-services agreement signed by all.

### 5. Dual Relationships and Boundary Issues

**Definitions:**
- **Multiple relationship:** When therapist/supervisor has concurrent or consecutive personal, social, business, or professional relationship with client/supervisee in addition to therapeutic/supervisory relationship, and these roles conflict or compete (Kitchener, 1988)
- **Boundary:** "The 'edge' of appropriate or professional behavior, transgression of which involves the therapist stepping out of the clinical role" (Gutheil & Simon, 2002)
- **Boundary crossing:** "Nonpejorative term describing departures from commonly accepted clinical practice that may or may not benefit the client" (Smith & Fitzpatrick, 1995) — may be harmless, nonexploitative, supportive of therapy
- **Boundary violation:** Departures from accepted practice that place client/supervisee/therapeutic process at serious risk — exploitation of the client (Gutheil & Gabbard, 1993)
- **"Slippery slopes":** Seemingly insignificant erosions in boundaries that transform into significant violations

**APA Ethical Standard 3.05(a):** "A psychologist refrains from entering into a multiple relationship if the multiple relationship could reasonably be expected to impair the psychologist's objectivity, competence, or effectiveness... or otherwise risk exploitation or harm... Multiple relationships that would not reasonably be expected to cause impairment or risk exploitation or harm are not unethical."

**APA Ethical Standard 7.07:** "Psychologists do not engage in sexual relationships with students or supervisees who are in their department, agency, or training center or over whom psychologists have or are likely to have evaluative authority." (Note: expanded from previous code to include broader center/agency.)

**ACES Ethical Guidelines 2.09:** "Supervisors who have multiple roles (e.g., teacher, clinical supervisor, administrative supervisor, etc.) with supervisees should minimize potential conflicts. Where possible, the roles should be divided among several supervisors. Where this is not possible, careful explanation should be conveyed to the supervisee as to the expectations and responsibilities associated with each supervisory role."

**Gutheil and Gabbard (1993) — Categories of boundary crossings/violations:**
- Role
- Time
- Place and space
- Money
- Gifts
- Services and related matters
- Clothing
- Language
- Self-disclosure and related matters
- Physical contact

**Lamb (2001) — Four levels of self-disclosure:**
1. Public information easily accessible to clients
2. Private information not known to clients (e.g., number of children)
3. Private events experienced by therapist (divorce, loss, trauma)
4. Personal opinions, beliefs, feelings related to client or client's concerns (including personal reactions to client)

**Areas NOT suitable for disclosure:** Details of current life stressors, dreams, fantasies, social/sexual/financial circumstances (Gutheil & Gabbard, 1993).

### 6. Sexual Misconduct: Prevalence, Prevention, and Risk Factors

**Prevalence:**
- 17% of female APA Division 12 members had sexual contact with psychology educators as graduate students (Glaser & Thorpe, 1986)
- 31% experienced sexual advances by teachers/supervisors during training or psychotherapy
- 10% student-educator sex reported more recently (Hammel, Olkin, & Taube, 1996)
- 1.4-4% of clinical supervisors have had sexual relationships with supervisees
- 5-6% of supervisees have been sexually involved with supervisors
- 80-88% of psychologists report having been attracted to/having sexual feelings for at least one client
- 34-45% of supervisees with sexual feelings toward clients did NOT discuss in supervision

**Hamilton and Spruill (1999) — Predictors of sexual boundary risk:**
- Loneliness
- Prior quasi "counseling" experience
- Professional inexperience
- Training factors: instruction on dealing with sexual feelings of attraction
- Failure to recognize ethical conflicts
- Failure of supervisors to supervise in these areas

**Brodsky (1989) — Risk factors increasing therapist-client sexual involvement:**
- Proneness to boundary crossings
- Situational vulnerability (personal stressor)
- Isolation

**Prevention strategy (Hamilton & Spruill, 1999):**
- Before trainees see clients: education (and self-disclosure) by faculty/supervisors on sexual attraction, how to deal with it, necessity of seeking supervision
- Learn warning signs and risk factors for therapist-client intimacy
- Learn harmful effects that can occur
- Social-skills training and role-playing to model and decrease anxiety
- Clear departmental policies on ethical infractions
- Differentiation between normally occurring feelings and unacceptable actions
- Clear case conceptualization as standard practice so supervisor can evaluate deviation

### 7. Kitchener (1988) — Model for Assessing Dual Role Risk

Potential for harm increases with:
1. **Increasing divergence** between the two individuals in the relationship
2. **Increasing incompatibility of expectations** between roles
3. **Divided loyalties and loss of objectivity** as obligations diverge
4. **Increasing power and prestige difference** — potential for exploitation and lack of objectivity widens
5. Areas of **sexual intimacy and money** are particularly high risk

### 8. Gottlieb (1993) — Decision-Making Model for Multiple Relationships

**Three dimensions to assess:**
1. **Power** — amount/degree of power psychologist has over client, potential for exploitation
2. **Duration** — power increases over time, so length of relationship matters
3. **Termination** — probability of further professional contact

**Five-step process:**
1. Assess current relationship according to three dimensions
2. Examine contemplated relationship along those three dimensions
3. Identify role incompatibilities
4. Obtain consultation from a colleague
5. Discuss decision with client/consumer, including ethical issues and possible adverse consequences

**Anderson and Kitchener (1998) critique:** Model may overlook therapist motivation, confidentiality, time elapsed between termination and new relationship, and effect on client's therapy progress.

### 9. Burian and Slimp (2000) — Decision-Making Model for Dual Relationships with Trainees

Three main issues to consider:
1. Each party's reasons and motivations to be in the relationship
2. Power differential between staff member and intern
3. Parameters of social activities

**LIKERT SCALES (Figure 7.1) — assess three areas with 5-point scales:**

**Intern's ability to leave social relationship without repercussions:**
1=Freely exit with no repercussions ... 5=Profound repercussions (intern leaving internship prematurely)

**Probable impact on uninvolved interns:**
1=No impact ... 5=Impaired service to clients/patients

**Probable impact on uninvolved staff members:**
1=No impact ... 5=Impaired service to clients/patients

If rating of 2+ on any scale: STOP or consult and proceed with caution.

**Decision Tree considerations:**
- Professional benefit of intern?
- Professional development of staff member?
- Personal benefit of intern?
- Personal benefit of staff member?
- Does activity take place more than once or twice per month?

### 10. Prevention of Boundary Violations — Psychoeducational Approaches

**Lamb (1999):**
- Orientation efforts to enhance bonding, provide support for stress, allow safe discussion of boundary dilemmas
- Clearly articulated channels to discuss, report, and deal with inappropriate behavior without fear of reprisal
- Seminars on self-care, stress reduction, identification of and dealing with boundary crossings/violations
- Role-playing or in vivo techniques strongly recommended

**Biaggio, Paget, and Chenoweth (1997) — Preventive model for faculty-student dual relationships:**
- Acknowledge power and responsibility of faculty
- Develop evaluative frame for faculty-student relationships to avoid exploitation
- Foster climate supporting ethical relationships with students
- Faculty role modeling of appropriate ethical behavior
- Safety in discussion of perceived boundary issues

**Gutheil and Gabbard (1993):**
- Teach limit-setting behavior when clients begin to cross boundaries
- Calm statements of inappropriateness in therapy context, followed by discussion and processing

### 11. General Ethical Problem-Solving Approaches

#### A. Koocher and Keith-Spiegel (1998) — 9-Step Model (derived from Tymchuk, Haas & Malouf):

1. Determine that the matter is an ethical one
2. Consult available ethical guidelines that might apply, to provide a possible mechanism for resolution
3. Consider all sources that might influence the decision you will make
4. Consult with a trusted colleague
5. Evaluate the rights, responsibilities, and vulnerability of all affected parties
6. Generate alternative decisions
7. Enumerate the consequences of making each decision
8. Make the decision
9. Implement the decision

#### B. Barret, Kitchener, and Burris (2001) — Systematic Ethical Decision-Making Model (originally for HIV, applied generally):

1. Pause and identify personal responses to the case
2. Review the facts
3. Conceptualize a preliminary plan
4. Analyze the plan in terms of professional codes of ethics, foundational ethical principles (autonomy, beneficence, do no harm, fidelity, justice — Kitchener, 1984) to determine whether plan is congruent
5. Examine the legal consequences of the preliminary plan
6. Refine the plan to balance clinical, ethical, and legal considerations so it:
   - Is congruent with one's personal values
   - Advances clinical interests as possible
   - Is operable within agency policies and professional ethics codes
   - Minimizes harm to client and relevant others
   - Maximizes the effect of all other ethical principles to greatest extent possible
   - Operates within the law
7. Choose a course of action and share it with the client
8. Implement the course of action, and monitor and document the outcomes

**Advantages:** Integration of emotional response, complete review format within multiple contexts. For supervisor-supervisee dilemmas, Steps 1 and 6 necessitate multilevel considerations of supervisor, supervisee, and client.

#### C. N.D. Hansen and Goldberg (1999) — Seven-Category Matrix:

Considerations as a matrix (not hierarchy) for greater flexibility:
1. Moral principles and personal values (competence, integrity, responsibility, respect, concern for others' welfare, social responsibility)
2. Clinical and cultural factors
3. Professional codes of ethics
4. Agency or employer policies
5. Federal, state, and local statutes
6. Rules and regulations
7. Case law

#### D. Kitchener (2000) — Protocol:
- Step 3: Generate possible options and alternative plans
- Step 7: Reassessment of options and identification of a plan
- Step 9: Reflection on the outcome of the decision for future application

### 12. Integrating Culture into Ethical Decision Making

**Ridley, Liddle, Hill, and Li (2001):**
- Encourage flexibility in representing ethical concerns
- Determine whether cultural conflict is implicit or explicit in the ethical issue
- Make implicit cultural assumptions explicit
- Factor these into decision-making process from both personal and systemic perspectives

**Pedersen (2002):** Neither APA's nor ACA's ethical guidelines are culturally sensitive — both are culturally encapsulated, guidelines for applications are vague, majority cultural values such as individualism serve as the norm.

**Lerman and Porter (1990) — Feminist perspective:** Existing ethical codes are reactive rather than proactive, dichotomize behavior, and ignore issues of minorities and women.

### 13. Rest (1984) — Four Components of Ethical Action

Failure to act ethically = deficiency in one of four components:
1. **Interpreting the situation as a moral one** — recognizing ethical dimensions in context of other aspects (clinical, pragmatic); how one's actions affect welfare of others
2. **Deciding which course of action is just, right, or fair** — moral reasoning to differentiate ethical from unethical choices
3. **Deciding what one intends to do** — choosing whether to carry out ethical action in face of competing forces
4. **Implementing the moral action** — carrying it out despite costs to self or external pressures; requires ego strength

### 14. Handelsman et al. (2002) — Professional Acculturation Model (Applied from Berry, 1990)

How trainees integrate personal morality with professional ethical standards:

Four categories:
1. **Professional assimilation:** Adopting professional psychology ethics and discarding one's own values
2. **Separation:** Maintaining own values with low identification with professional code
3. **Marginalization:** Both undeveloped personal moral sense and low internalized professional ethical sense
4. **Integration:** Both maintaining one's moral/ethical values AND adopting professional codes (desired outcome)

Unless trainees integrate their own ethical identity with professional psychology's, they may be at higher risk for ethical difficulty. Trainees' personal ethical/moral codes should be encouraged, highlighted, and valued.

### 15. Competence

**Pope and Vasquez (1998) — Two types of competence:**
1. **Intellectual competence:** Education and knowledge of research, interventions, treatment efficacy, theory, critical thinking, conceptualization. Critical part: awareness of what one does NOT know.
2. **Emotional competence:** Knowledge of self, monitoring of self, acceptance of self — relevant to supervisee training in self-care.

**APA Ethical Standard 2.01(a):** "Psychologists provide services, teach, and conduct research with populations and in areas only within the boundaries of their competence based on their education, training, supervised experience, consultation, study, or professional experience."

**APA Ethical Standard 2.05:** "Psychologists who delegate work to ... supervisees ... take reasonable steps to ... (2) authorize only those responsibilities that such persons can be expected to perform competently on the basis of their education, training, or experience, either independently or with the level of supervision being provided and (3) see that such persons perform these services competently."

**Haas and Malouf (1989) — Guidelines for assessing own competence:**
- Existence of relevant standards
- Grounding of approach in research or theory
- Contextual constraints
- Whether one is emotionally able to be of help
- Whether one's decision could be justified to a group of one's peers

**A.S. Newman (1981) — Components of supervisory competence:**
- Training in theory and practice of supervision
- Experience in practicing the skills they help trainees learn
- Training or experience in assessment or treatment of types of client problems the trainee is seeing

**ACES Ethical Guidelines 3.02:** "Supervisors should teach courses and supervise clinical work only in areas where they are fully competent and experienced."

### 16. Legal Liability of Supervisors (Vicarious Liability)

**Respondent superior (vicarious liability):** "One who occupies a position of authority or direct control over another (such as a master and servant, employer and employee, or supervisor and supervisee) can be held legally liable for the damages of another suffered as a result of the negligence of the subordinate" (Disney & Stephens, 1994).

**Direct liability:** Arises from supervisor's inattention to trainee's comments or failing to carry out supervision.

**"Four D's" of malpractice (Behnke et al., 1998):**
Dereliction of a Duty Directly causing Damages.
All four must be present by a preponderance of evidence.

**Bennett et al. (1990) — Four criteria of malpractice:**
1. A professional relationship was formed between psychologist and client — only thus does practitioner incur legal duty of care
2. There is a demonstrable standard of care, and practitioner breached that standard (practiced "below the standard of care")
3. The client suffered harm or injury, which must be demonstrated and established
4. The practitioner's breach of duty was the proximate cause of the client's injury (reasonably foreseeable consequence)

**Factors for supervisory malpractice determination (Disney & Stephens, 1994):**
1. Supervisor's power to control the supervisee (administrative authority or ability to gain knowledge)
2. Supervisee's duty to perform the act (failure as reasonable supervisor would assume)
3. Time, place, and purpose of the act (during formal supervision, within course/scope of relationship)
4. Motivation of supervisee for committing the act (beneficence or malfeasance)
5. Whether supervisor could have reasonably expected supervisee would commit the act

**"Borrowed-servant" rule:** When university places student in community setting, liability may be shared. University = general employer; placement site = special employer. Determine which employer had control by examining affiliation agreements. If no agreements exist, university may be liable as general employer (Saccuzzo, 2002).

**Key legal cases:**
- **Tarasoff v. Regents of University of California (1976):** Established duty to warn. Supervisor found liable along with psychologist for failing to take steps to warn and protect.
- **Hedlund v. Superior Court of Orange County (1983):** Extended definition of victim to anyone foreseeably at risk.
- **Jablonski v. United States (1983):** Failure to obtain past medical records; highlighted critical nature of communication among professionals.
- **Peck v. Counseling Service of Addison County (1985):** Counselor did not take client's threat seriously, did not consult supervisor. Center found negligent for no written policies/procedures.
- **Almonte v. New York Medical College (1994):** Supervisor has duty to clients of supervisee if reasonable foreseeability of harm. Psychoanalyst/school faculty member had responsibility to inform school about resident's pedophilia.
- **Cohen v. State of New York (1975):** Unsupervised resident released suicidal client. Court found resident did not possess enough skill/judgment for reasonable treatment.
- **Andrews v. United States (1982):** Supervisor did not adequately investigate report of sexual relationship; found liable. Had supervision been proper, relationship would have been terminated and harm prevented.

### 17. Duty to Warn and Protect — Compiled Steps

(Compiled from Herlihy & Sheeley, 1988; VandeCreek & Knapp, 2001):

1. Warn the intended victim
2. Increase frequency of treatment
3. Refer for changing or adding appropriate medication
4. Make plans to eliminate access of client to lethal weapon
5. Deal with aggression in the treatment
6. Subject client to involuntary commitment
7. Notify the police
8. Consult with colleagues
9. Document every step of every action the supervisor and supervisee take

**Key considerations:**
- Duty to protect arises only when victim is identifiable, could be identifiable after "a moment's reflection," or is part of an identifiable class of reasonably foreseeable victims
- Supervisee must hear communication of specific threat of serious physical violence (with intent and ability) against reasonably identifiable victim
- "Duty to warn, protect, and predict" (Behnke et al., 1998)

**Chenneville (2000):** Guidelines for duty to protect in case of HIV.

**Risk assessment and management training needed:**
- State-of-the-art risk assessment techniques (Rosenberg, 1999)
- Management of violent clients (Beck, 1987; Borum, 1996; Tishler, Gordon, & Landry-Meyer, 2000)
- Suicide risk assessment (Kleespies, Penk, & Forsyth, 1993)
- Guidelines for working with potentially violent clients (VandeCreek & Knapp, 1993)

**Kleespies et al. (1993):** 40% of psychology trainees experienced client suicide or attempt during training. Earlier in training = more traumatic. Preparation essential.

### 18. Documentation Standards for Supervision

**Falvey (2002):** "Documentation is no longer an option in supervision."

**Documentation includes:**
- Supervisory contract
- Supervisee's application materials (or minimum CV)
- All performance evaluations conducted
- Monitoring log containing:
  - List of cases supervisee is carrying
  - Dates of supervision of each
  - When supervision actually occurred
  - Presentation of problems and critical issues
  - Directives/directions supervisee is following in treatment
  - Changes in diagnosis or treatment plan
  - Discussions of case progress
  - Details of safety, ethical, legal, or risk management concerns raised and their resolution
  - Follow-up reports on previous interventions or concerns
  - Details of supervisory or supervisee problems and their resolution
  - Supervisee attendance records and make-up information
- Notation if past/present medical records, audiotapes, videotapes, or assessment reports are reviewed
- Termination summary: circumstances of termination, recommendations/referrals, follow-up by supervisee

**Qualities of notes:** Objective, nonjudgmental, nonderogatory, clear, concrete (Stromberg et al., 1988).

**Retention:** As long as clinical records required in state — anywhere from 5 to 30 years (Falvey, 2002). APA: minimum 3 years after last contact; records/summary then maintained additional 12 years before disposal.

### 19. Letters of Recommendation

**Ethical considerations:**
- Informed-consent issue: students waive rights to review letter before knowing what contents will be
- "Letter inflation" — predominance of extremely glowing, exaggerated letters (Range et al., 1991)
- Balanced, accurate letter can appear negative because of inflation
- Even predominantly positive letter with ANY negative evaluation likely results in applicant's rejection
- 53% of supervisors believed they had written biased letters (Robiner, Saltzman, Hoberman, Semrud-Clikeman, & Schirvar, 1997)

**Grote et al. (2001) — Recommendations:**
- Use Likert scales rather than binary yes-no decisions
- Acknowledge that "no applicant is perfect" and describe areas of personal or professional growth
- 46.5% would refuse to write letter for student with known alcohol/drug abuse problems
- 12.4% would exclude all mention of alcohol/drug problems

**R.K. Miller and Van Rybroek (1988) — Helpful letter elements:**
- Caveat about how many letters overstate desirable qualities
- Highlight strengths AND weaknesses in specific skills, experience, commitments, traits
- Examples/reasons individual should be selected over others
- Suggestions of types of internship experiences candidate might most benefit from

**Recommendations:**
- Standardization with details on scope of experience with supervisee
- Mention of relationship with supervisee (primary supervisor, director of training)
- Description of context of relationship
- Discussion of special skills or attributes
- Clear description of what was supervised
- If unable to write positive letter: should have been dealt with in ongoing supervision; must be clearly discussed with supervisee

### 20. Gatekeeping Responsibilities

**ACES Ethical Guidelines 2.12:**
"Supervisors, through ongoing supervisee assessment and evaluation, should be aware of any personal or professional limitations of supervisees which are likely to impede future professional performance. Supervisors have the responsibility of recommending remedial assistance to the supervisee and of screening from the training program, applied counseling setting, or state licensure those supervisees who are unable to provide competent professional services. These recommendations should be clearly and professionally explained in writing to the supervisees who are so evaluated."

**Gatekeeping intersects with:**
- Supervisory contract (see Chapter 8 for full details)
- Evaluation procedures
- Due process
- Competency assessment

### 21. How to Handle a Supervisee in Crisis or Ethical Violation

**Lamb et al. (1991) — Four-Step Model for Responding to Trainee Problematic Behavior:**

**Step 1:** Supervisors who have identified problematic areas consult to clarify concerns. Consultations, modifications to trainee's program, and concerns themselves must be documented following due-process procedure. Trainee given ample chance to remediate deficiencies and provide formal reply. Institute remediation plan with specific timelines and clearly articulated performance expectations.

**Step 2:** If Step 1 measures don't produce improvement: Additional documentation and discussion of problematic behaviors. Training staff implements full range of interventions and observes impact. Modifications made to supervision procedure itself. Further elements of supervision and other interventions added as deemed necessary. Notification of trainee's school at this point (or earlier).

**Step 3:** Consider expulsion from program. If probation necessary: intern notified in writing. Information shared with academic institution. Due-process steps followed including appeal-process steps. If expulsion occurs: school and trainee simultaneously notified; process and time for departure planned.

**Step 4:** Address impact on all remaining trainees, staff members, academic program, and the trainee him/herself.

**Slimp and Burian (1994) — Prevention strategies:**
- Establish internal ethics committee comprising both interns and staff
- Have ethics consultant unaffiliated with program, or use local/APA ethics committee
- Establish policies/procedures for: how to consult ethics committee, reasons it may be consulted, procedures for remediation and sanctions, whether committee recommendations are binding, methods for formal actions

### 22. Risk Management — Summary of Protective Steps

1. Adherence to informed consent
2. Competency (functioning within areas of expertise)
3. Confidentiality
4. Due process
5. Duty to warn
6. Taking reasonable steps to think through and document the process
7. "Do what a reasonable supervisor would do under the particular set of circumstances"
8. Ethical problem-solving models (consultation with colleague particularly important)
9. "Worst case thinking" (Harris, 2002) — considering worst possible outcomes, especially during high uncertainty or crisis

**Sherry (1991) — Supervisory competence assessment includes:**
- All supervisees should evaluate their supervision and provide feedback to supervisors
- Progress toward supervisory contract goals should be reviewed regularly
- Supervision of supervisors should be provided
- Supervisors should self-assess, taking into account successes, failures, and areas for growth

### 23. Competencies for Ethical and Legal Considerations

- Supervisors function within their areas of competency in all clinical practice and supervision
- Supervisors have knowledge and training in ethics of mental health professions and conduct themselves in a manner that models such behavior. This competency encompasses all areas of ethical functioning, including boundary definition
- Supervisors have knowledge of laws and regulations of the mental health disciplines and conduct themselves accordingly. They seek consultation as needed
- Supervisors develop goals and contracts with supervisees and translate these objectives into training, giving ongoing constructive feedback and evaluation and documenting all parts of the process

## CHAPTER 8: EVALUATION OF THE SUPERVISORY PROCESS

### 1. Formative vs. Summative Evaluation

**Formative evaluation:**
- Viewed as a way to assist in skill refinement and identification of personal issues that may be impeding clinical practice
- Viewed more positively than summative
- Because psychologists believe in positive change and power of feedback, formative evaluation may be syntonic
- Ongoing, process-oriented

**Summative evaluation:**
- Objective assessment of competence and performance
- Not viewed as positively
- Outcomes: completion and passing, probationary status, or termination
- Greater ambivalence or negativity associated with this form
- To some supervisors, being an evaluator may feel like a dual role: supportive/personal/growth-focused vs. giving hard facts on how trainee is not measuring up

**Ronnestad and Skovholt (1993) distinction:**
- **Confirmatory feedback** given to beginning trainees
- **Corrective feedback** given to more advanced trainees

**Within larger categories, multiple levels of evaluation:**
- Evaluative feedback
- Corrective feedback
- General feedback

### 2. ALL Evaluation Methods and Tools

**Types of evaluation listed in the chapter:**

1. **Qualitative or quantitative evaluation of material presented by supervisee** regarding client and interactions between client and supervisee-therapist, based on recollection (described by Cone [2001] as indirect, because of temporal gap and lack of direct observation)

2. **Qualitative or quantitative evaluation of observational (live or videotaped) data** of interactions between client/family and supervisee-therapist (described by Cone [2001] as direct, no temporal gap), which may include responses to variables that can be operationalized and coded or Likert scales assessing:
   - Affective component
   - Cultural and diversity competency
   - Relationship and interpersonal skills
   - Conceptualization
   - Diagnosis
   - Technical skills
   - Adherence to the model
   - Empathy

3. **Evaluation of progress of supervisee toward predesignated therapy goals**

4. **Analysis of client-report outcome data** on client's progress

5. **Use of rating scales** for role induction or to structure formative feedback

6. **Use of unobtrusive measures:**
   - Comparing tallies of client attendance and cancellations
   - Analyzing rates of client retention
   - Determining number of clients seen per week or psychodiagnostics sessions completed
   - Assessing supervisee timely and credible completion of paperwork

7. **Analysis of client satisfaction data** based on client reports and attendance records

8. **Assessment of case presentations** at grand rounds or diagnostic team meetings

9. **Analysis of portfolios or examinations** (adapted from Sumerall et al., 2000)

10. **Ratings of a work sample** including diagnostic assessment, case formulation, and course of treatment (Dienst & Armstrong, 1988)

11. **Analysis of critical incidents** (Norman, 1985) — incidents that were major turning points in supervision, resulting in change to supervisee's approach or effectiveness

12. **Study of diagnostic thinking, clinical judgment, or problem solving** — physicians observed and questioned as they solve a clinical problem (Norman, 1985)

13. **360-degree (multirater) feedback** — ratings from complete circle of viewpoints: peers, managers, clients, direct reports (Sala & Dwight, 2002). Evidence shows:
    - Self-ratings have very low correlations with others' perceptions
    - Peer, direct-report, and managerial ratings correlate moderately
    - Self-ratings tend to be more lenient than supervisor ratings
    - More feedback-seeking behavior = more congruent self-ratings with supervisor ratings
    - High convergence between 360-degree ratings and self-ratings = self-awareness (Church, 1997)

14. **Rubrics** (Hanna & Smith, 1998) — quantitative method for assessment of students' performance targets against agreed-on standards:
    - Each dimension clearly described
    - Scale with very specific examples of achievement at each level
    - Example: competency "establishing counseling relationship" — highest rating 5 = "At least 97% of the time, makes a conscious effort to use core conditions (empathy, unconditional positive regard, genuineness, concreteness/intentionality)"; lowest rating 1 = "has significant difficulty exhibiting knowledge or use of core conditions"

15. **Microcounseling paradigm** (Daniels, Rigazio-Diglio, & Ivey, 1997) — built-in evaluation from definition of baseline levels with predetermined criteria; hierarchy of skills from basic attending to individualizing personal style and theory

16. **Supervisor Adherence Measure** (Henggeler et al., 2002) — questionnaire articulating and assessing multiple aspects of supervisor's role in implementing a manualized treatment model (e.g., multisystemic therapy/MST)

**Specific instruments:**

- **Frame and Stevens-Smith (1995) — Nine characteristics for counselor development** (on 5-point Likert, minimum 3 per item): openness, flexibility, positivity, cooperativeness, willingness to use and accept feedback, awareness of one's impact on others, ability to deal with conflict, ability to accept personal responsibility, ability to express feelings effectively and appropriately

- **Process Evaluation of Teaching and Supervision** (Milne & James, 2002) — coding supervisee behaviors (reflecting, experimenting, conceptualizing, experiencing, planning) in context of supervisor behaviors (managing, listening, supporting, informing/educating, guiding experiential learning)

- **Evaluation Process Within Supervision Inventory** (Lehrman-Waterman & Ladany, 2001; see Appendix I) — self-report, 7-point Likert. Two groupings: goal setting (e.g., "My supervisor and I created goals that were realistic") and feedback (e.g., "The feedback I received was directly related to the goals we established"). Correlated but conceptually distinct.

- **Supervisory Utilization Rating Form (SURF)** (Vespia et al., 2002) — tool for role induction; helps supervisees define their task; identifies differences between importance assigned by supervisors and supervisees

- **Process Evaluation of Teaching and Supervision** (Milne & James, 2002) — another format for assessment

- **Worthen and Isakson (2000) measure** (Appendix J) — supervisee introspection on: suitability, conceptualization, relationship, technique, results, and satisfaction with supervision

- **Outcome Questionnaire (OQ-45)** — self-report with three subscales: symptom distress, interpersonal relations, social role performance. Youth version (Y-OQ) for ages 4-17: six subscales (intrapersonal distress, somatics, interpersonal relations, critical items, social problems, behavioral dysfunction). Methodology combines OQ-45 with therapeutic alliance assessment or analysis of client's motivation for change.

- **Color-coded feedback system (Lambert & Hawkins, 2001):** White = normal functioning (termination needed); Green = adequate progress (no change needed); Yellow = less than adequate progress (treatment plan change needed); Red = client not progressing at expected level (may withdraw prematurely or have negative effect)

- **Clement (1999) scales** — fixed behavioral points for client self-monitoring, usable in supervision

- **Professional Counseling Performance Evaluation** (Kerl, Garcia, McCullough, & Maxwell, 2002) — includes maturity, competence, integrity; items on self-control, honesty, fairness, respect

### 3. How to Give Effective Feedback (Specific Techniques)

**Reflective:**
- "I wonder how that felt to you."
- "I wonder what you were thinking or feeling when..."
- Reflection on the impact of a particular client on the supervisee
- Observation of a behavior or event that was unnoticed in the session
- A general observation about the role of the therapist vis a vis the clients

**Integrative:**
- "Let's think about that and how it fits in the direction in which you see the family moving"
- "Where would pursuing that option have led?"
- "What was your thought process when you let that opportunity pass?"

**Neutral:** "It would be interesting to know..."

**Empowering:**
- "Your skill set has markedly enlarged, and with it your integration of clinical material"
- "Your use of the metaphor far surpasses your self-assessment of play-therapy competence"
- "Think about how well you approached the same problem last month when the mother was so angry"
- "Remember how your research experience with anxiety provides you with a perspective on how to approach this situation"
- "I am struck by the contrast between how self-effacing you are in supervision and how effective you are with this family"
- "Your skillful use of the metaphor paved the way for the child's disclosure about her level of pain"

**Analytic:**
- "Let's analyze the pattern of what has been happening in the past three sessions"
- "I'm wondering what your thought process would have been if there were any other ways to have linked that situation to the history of abuse"

**Responsive:**
- "It seemed like you were wondering which way to proceed"
- "What emotions were elicited in you when your client accused you of being unfeeling?"

**Corrective:**
- "It would be interesting to see what prefaces besides disclaimers you could use"
- "I am wondering what other ideas you and I can come up with to get around the problem of the mother's continuing to ignore the child's appropriate behavior"

**Level-I Concern** (specification of concern and context):
- "I'm concerned that the mother started to broach punishment and you changed the subject. Why do you think that happened?"

**Level-II Concern:**
- "The fact that you did not follow up with the input about exploring possible child abuse in this case is a legal breach as well as a breach of professionalism"

**Evaluative feedback — Positive:**
- "You are responding much more clearly and empathically to this family, a direction we indicated in your goals. Good job"
- "Your intervention had the desired effect on the adolescent"

**Evaluative feedback — Negative:**
- "You are continuing to ignore affective expression of this child. Attending to the child's affective expression is a goal in your contract, and I am increasingly concerned that the efforts we are making to improve this area are not succeeding"
- "I am continuing to be concerned about how you do not seem to be integrating the concept of process in your clinical work. For your level of training and experience, I am very surprised that you are not progressing more easily"

**General guidelines for feedback delivery:**
- Use strength-based approach, but be honest
- "Emphasis on strengths to the extent that areas needing improvement are not mentioned is not an ethical, creditable, or educational approach"
- Some seasoned supervisors prefer to give very positive feedback throughout most of the supervisory hour and, at most, one negative or corrective piece of feedback toward the end, interwoven with statements of trainee's strengths
- Goals clearly defined (as in training contract) — tangible behavioral feedback linked to training goals
- In case of trainee continuing to be content-oriented instead of process-oriented: identify shifts to content, analyze feeling state and thinking process at these junctures, analyze alternative explanations of why shifts occurred
- Supervisors must have competency in providing formative and summative feedback

### 4. How to Handle Evaluation Anxiety in Supervisees

- Evaluation should be viewed simply as part of the effective learning process — not punitive and worrisome
- Trainees view high levels of feedback and evaluation as highly desirable (multiple studies: Gandolfo & Brown, 1987; Henderson et al., 1999; Leddick & Dye, 1987; Miller & Getting, 1966; Nerdrum & Ronnestad, 2002)
- Effective evaluation practices are positively associated with and predictive of a stronger working alliance (Lehrman-Waterman & Ladany, 2001)
- Unidirectional evaluation (supervisor to supervisee) creates more anxiety than bidirectional
- Supervisees' evaluations of supervisors would potentially provide supervisors with feedback on their own performance
- Perceived incongruity between developing supportive supervisory relationship and then giving negative feedback
- Trainees may not self-disclose relevant personal information out of fear it will adversely affect evaluation (Ladany, Ellis, et al., 1999)
- "Liking" one's supervisor may be related to the evaluative process
- Most effective supervision may NOT be the most satisfying supervision — supervisors who give serious feedback may not be trainee favorites at the time feedback is given
- Client-feedback approach (using OQ-45) is value neutral and easier for trainees to tolerate and incorporate (Worthen & Isakson, 2002)

### 5. Evaluation Criteria and Competency Benchmarks

**Ellis and Ladany (1997) — Parameters for evaluating supervisee competence:**
- Scope of competency
- Particular domain and modality of therapy behavior
- Skills and behaviors desired
- Designated period of time
- Method of assessment

**Rater bias types (Robiner, Saltzman, Hoberman, Semrud-Clikeman, et al., 1997):**
- Leniency (ratings more positive than warranted)
- Central tendency (ratings skewed more toward average than warranted)
- Strictness (ratings more negative than warranted)
- 58% of supervisors believed their evaluations were biased in some way

**Cormier and Bernard (1982) — Three pivotal skills of psychologist as evaluator:**
1. Ability to evaluate efficiently using objective criteria as a yardstick or measure
2. Ability to communicate feedback clearly and have method of checking counselor's understanding of feedback
3. Ability to be comfortable and effective with the power inherent in the role of supervisor

### 6. The Supervision Contract (Training Contract/Working Agreement)

**Key elements (Proctor, 1997; Cobia & Boes, 2000; Osborne & Davis, 1996; Ronnestad & Skovholt, 1993):**

Should be:
- In writing (Osborne & Davis, 1996)
- Initiated by supervisor (Sherry, 1991) prior to or at very beginning of training sequence
- Signed by both supervisor and supervisee, copies for both (Bridge & Bascue, 1990)
- Brought to supervision regularly for reference/review

**Contents:**
1. **Purpose** of training
2. **Goals and objectives** — mutually agreed upon
   - How progress toward goals will be evaluated
   - Expected periods of time for accomplishment
   - Responsibilities if goals not met (Cobia & Boes, 2000)
   - Qualities: specificity, realistic quality, measurability, prioritization, early definition (Lehrman-Waterman & Ladany, 2001)
3. **Context of services** — opportunities provided within setting, unique characteristics of clients
4. **Evaluation methods** — amount, type (formal vs. informal, written vs. verbal), timing, frequency
   - Specify that "primary role of supervisor is that of evaluation" (Osborne & Davis, 1996)
   - Methods by which information is recorded (formal evaluation format, verbal feedback, narrative letter)
   - Parties receiving parts of evaluation and which parts
5. **Duties and responsibilities** of both supervisor and supervisee
   - Supervisor agrees to: follow all ethical, legal, and office codes/policies; be available; identify coverage for absences; document supervision; provide evaluation
   - Supervisee agrees to: document supervised activities; follow all ethical, legal, and office codes/policies; inform clients of status as unlicensed supervisee under supervision
6. **Procedural considerations** — notes, taping, conflict resolution, how supervision conducted, types of monitoring activities
7. **Definition of supervisor's scope of competency**
8. **Logistics** — amount and length of supervision, location, cancellation procedures
9. **Supervisor's theoretical orientation(s)** for therapy and supervision
10. **Payment arrangements**, malpractice insurance, record-keeping expectations

**Methods that need to be spelled out:**
- Reviewing required videotapes, audiotapes, or live observations
- Supervisory interventions: role-playing, midsession supervision with feedback, didactics, observation of clinician/supervisor
- Live supervision serves as ethical safeguard (Cormier & Bernard, 1982)

**Sutter, McPherson, and Geeseman (2002) prototype:** Covers structural, ethical, and procedural aspects but not qualitative, individualized, or programmatic factors relating to growth/development.

**Osborn and Davis (1996) outline:** Includes more qualitative/developmental factors but does not lay out competencies or specific individualized training goals.

**Ronnestad and Skovholt (1993) — Balancing goals:** General enough to be meaningful, specific enough to allow assessment. Dangers: changing goals too frequently OR maintaining goals too rigidly.

**Talen and Schindler (1993) — "Goal-directed supervision plans":**
- Goals relating to: conceptual models, therapy skills, diagnosis/treatment planning, personal/professional issues
- Secondary benefit: enhancement of supervisory relationship and facilitation of evaluation
- Supervision strategies included: supervisory integration of data, expansion of trainee alternative responses, use of case examples to amplify particular intervention plan

**Example in Appendix A** — not intended to be exemplary, just to serve as example.

### 7. How to Document Evaluation

- APA Guidelines and Principles for Accreditation (2002e): "At the time of admission, the program provides interns with written policies and procedures regarding program requirements and expectations for interns' performance and continuance in the program and procedures for the termination of students."
- Interns receive, at least semiannually, written feedback on the extent to which they are meeting requirements and performance expectations
- Feedback should address: professional conduct, psychological knowledge, skills and competencies in assessment, intervention, and consultation
- Feedback should include: timely written notification of all problems noted and opportunity to discuss them; guidance regarding steps to remediate all problems (if remediable); substantive written feedback on extent to which corrective actions are or are not successful
- Standardized procedures, known to both parties from onset
- Specification of methods by which information is recorded (formal format, verbal feedback to school, narrative letter)
- Identification of parties receiving parts of evaluation and which parts
- Clear limits of confidentiality established (Osborn & Davis, 1996)

### 8. Handling Evaluation Disagreements

The chapter emphasizes:
- Due-process procedures should be followed
- Appeal-process steps available if trainee chooses to appeal
- Trainee given proper notice and opportunity for hearing, defense, and appeal
- Procedures used by supervisors may NOT be arbitrary, capricious, prejudicial, or discriminatory (Knoff & Prout, 1985)
- Consider using 360-degree feedback to reduce reliance on single supervisor's judgment
- Supervisee should have opportunity to provide formal reply
- Differences in perception between trainees and supervisors about degree of supportiveness of environment should be explored
- Supervisees may be reluctant to give feedback on supervisor weaknesses/deficiencies — any reports should be viewed tentatively

### 9. When and How to Give Negative Evaluations

**Context:**
- Most evaluations are positive, making it even more difficult to provide negative ones (Robiner, Saltzman, Hoberman, Semrud-Clikeman, et al., 1997)
- Negative evaluation may lead to remediation plans and possibly culminate in dismissal (Olkin & Gaughen, 1991), adding role of judge to supervisor

**Approach:**
- Link negative feedback directly to goals in training contract
- Be specific and behavioral
- Document thoroughly
- Provide clear remediation plan with timelines
- Ensure due process (notice, hearing, defense, appeal)
- Follow Lamb et al. (1991) four-step model
- Focus solely on specifically describing inadequate performance or behavior rather than looking for explanatory factors
- Use strength-based orientation with emphasis on small increments of improvement
- Even when negative evaluation is necessary, some very seasoned supervisors prefer to give positive feedback throughout most of the hour and, at most, one negative or corrective piece toward the end

**Legal considerations:**
- Professionally related interpersonal skills are within academic domain (Greenhill v. Bailey, 1975)
- Remedial measures are within domain of program's faculty if relevant to identified areas of deficit (Shuffer v. Trustees of California State University and Colleges, 1977)
- Board of Curators of the University of Missouri v. Horowitz (1978): Dismissal of medical student for deficient clinical performance, peer/patient relationships, and personal hygiene upheld. All rights under 14th Amendment sufficiently protected.
- Alanis v. University of Texas Health Sciences Center (1992): Unsuitability to practice medicine = academic reason for dismissal

### 10. Self-Evaluation Methods for Supervisors

**Why evaluate supervisors:**
- All supervisees should evaluate their supervision and provide feedback to supervisors (Sherry, 1991)
- Supervisors should self-assess, taking into account successes, failures, and areas for growth
- Supervisors should use this feedback to improve their own performance

**Suggested by Dendinger and Kohn (1989) — Multisource evaluations:**
- Self-evaluations
- Evaluations by the supervisor of the supervisor (meta-supervision)
- Evaluations by fellow supervisors (peer evaluation)
- Evaluations by supervisees
- Evaluations by individuals outside agency (expert assessment of supervisor's skill base)
- Administrators evaluate productivity, error rate, complaints

**Specific instruments for supervisor evaluation:**

1. **Supervision Satisfaction Questionnaire** (Ladany, Hill, Corbett, & Nutt, 1996) — addresses overall satisfaction without specific feedback about areas of less satisfaction

2. **Supervisee Perceptions of Supervision** (Oik & Friedlander, 1992; see Appendix H) — combines supervisee perceptions of supervision with assessments of level of uncertainty elicited. Also includes Role Conflict and Role Ambiguity Inventory.

3. **Supervisee/Trainee Self Assessment of Supervision-Related Knowledge and Skills** (Borders & Leddick, 1987; see Appendix G) — breaks down items into teaching, counseling, consultation, and research-skills components. The best form for gathering feedback specific to particular tasks.

4. **Hall-Marley form** (modified by Falender, Appendix L) — linked to overall expectations for supervisee competencies

5. **Hall-Marley supervisor feedback form** (Appendix K) — gathers supervisor feedback organized to coordinate with program goals and training contract

6. **Herrmann (1996) — Three-part format for medical education:**
   - Part 1: Critical-incident approach — number of hours spent with supervisor, number of live/taped interviews, number of times detailed feedback received
   - Part 2: Residents rate supervisors on: enthusiasm, organization, clarity, knowledge, clinical supervision skills, availability, modeling ability (with descriptive factors listed: role modeling, regular feedback, constructive criticism, sensitivity to gender/ethical/cultural/SES issues). Overall rating on 5-point Likert (poor to excellent)
   - Part 3: Narrative comments on supervisor's strengths, major weaknesses, and areas of improvement
   - Process: Return forms to program office with cover sheet identifying resident (removed and destroyed once checked in), as requirement for completion of rotation. Dramatically increased response rate. Anonymity allowed in large programs. Very positive outcomes from feedback given.

7. **Ramsbottom-Lucier, Gillmore, Irby, and Ramsey (1994) — Medical education format:**
   - Residents evaluate clinical supervisors on: knowledge and analytic ability, accessibility, establishment of rapport, organization and clarity, enthusiasm, involvement of learner, feedback and direction, demonstration of clinical skills (6-point Likert)
   - Residents also rate perceived degree of involvement (4-point scale)
   - High reliability scores, especially in outpatient settings

### 11. Trainee with Problematic Behavior (Definition and Identification)

**Lamb et al. (1991) — Definition of impairment in training context:**
"An interference in professional functioning that is reflected in one or more of the following ways:
(a) an inability or unwillingness to acquire and integrate professional standards into one's repertoire of professional behavior;
(b) an inability to acquire professional skills to reach an acceptable level of competency;
(c) an inability to control personal stress, psychological dysfunction and/or excessive emotional reactions that may affect professional functioning."

**Lamb et al. (1986) — Differentiation of competent from problematic trainees:**
(a) The problematic intern does NOT acknowledge, understand, or address the problem when it is identified
(b) The problem is NOT merely a reflection of a skill deficit that can be rectified by academic or didactic training
(c) The quality of services delivered is consistently negatively affected
(d) The problem is NOT restricted to one area of professional functioning
(e) A disproportionate amount of attention by training personnel is required
(f) The intern's behavior does NOT change as a function of feedback, remediation efforts, and/or time

**Overholser and Fine (1990) — Types of professional incompetence:**
- Lack of knowledge
- Inadequate clinical skills
- Deficient technical skills
- Poor judgment
- Disturbing interpersonal attributes

**Areas that may lead to remediation:**
- Ethical violation
- Psychopathology
- Poor academic performance
- Inadequate therapy (clinical) skills
- Inadequate assessment skills
- Poor clinical judgment
- Poor interpersonal skills
- Problematic response to supervision
- Poor theoretical skills
- Immaturity

**Rates:** Trainees with problematic behavior annually range from 3.3% to 4.8%. 66% of programs reported at least one such intern in past 5 years. Percentage of practicing psychologists with significant problematic issues estimated at 15-27%, with 7-14% not seeking help.

### 12. Remediation Methods

**Wise, Lowery, and Silverglade (1989):**
- Skill development
- Workshops
- Supervision groups
- Consultation
- Continuing-education experiences
- If still need to deal with specific issues or skill development blocked by personal issues: suggest personal counseling
- Optimal times for referral: before seeing clients; when identity as therapist has been partially established and trainee is gaining greater insight

**Other remediation methods:**
- Case-load modifications
- Intensive interventions such as live observation
- Cotherapy with supervisor
- Required supplemental courses
- Leaves of absence (Lamb et al., 1987)
- Goal setting and provision of more frequent feedback (Lehrman-Waterman & Ladany, 2001)
- Personal therapy and additional clinical field experiences (Knoff & Prout, 1985)

**Note:** It is NOT clear that therapy is the remediation of choice for academic or clinical functioning problems (Olkin & Gaughen, 1991). Therapy goals/timelines may be unclear; confidentiality means program will not be privy to trainee's progress.

### 13. Prevention of Problematic Behavior

**Coster and Schwebel (1997) — Interventions:**
- Interpersonal support: peers, spouse, companions, family, friends
- Intrapersonal activities: self-awareness monitoring
- Reduction of workload, increase of leisure activities and sleep
- Heightening awareness of professional life cycle
- Professional and civic activity and activism
- Self-care

**Lamb (1999) — Preventative strategies:**
- Comprehensive orientation with social networking, bonding opportunities
- Supportive environment
- Distribution of specific written guidelines about performance expectations and relationship to problematic behavior
- Open avenues for challenging evaluations or dealing with boundary issues
- Seminars on self-help and stressors, including means of dealing with them
- Guidelines for addressing boundary dilemmas
- Opportunities to identify and address boundary issues through role-playing or other means

**Guy and Norcross (1998):** Excellent self-care checklist.

### 14. Evaluation of the Supervisor's Own Performance

Methods discussed include:
- Self-assessment
- Feedback from supervisees (using instruments described above)
- Peer supervision and review
- Supervision of supervision (meta-supervision)
- Review of progress toward supervisory contract goals
- Analysis of supervisee benchmarks achieved during training

**Herrmann (1996) process:** Having supervisees return evaluation forms to program office as requirement for completion dramatically increased number of evaluations returned. Cover sheet identifying resident removed and destroyed once form checked in. Size of training program allowed anonymity.

### 15. Competencies for Evaluation

- Ability to administer a format for assessment of incoming supervisees, to identify their strengths and areas that need improvement, as well as a plan for their development during the training period
- Ability to construct a supervision contract
- Ability to construct tools to assess the supervisee's performance and behavior during the training sequence
- Skill at self-assessment for oneself and for administering self-assessment measures for the supervisee
- Skill at providing formative and summative feedback on an ongoing basis
- Skill at eliciting feedback about oneself from supervisees and using this feedback

## CHAPTER 9: THE EVOLVING PRACTICE OF CLINICAL SUPERVISION

### 1. Future Directions in Supervision

**Supervision as a distinct specialty:**
- Distinguish supervision from clinical practice
- Elevate its status as a unique specialty requiring education and training
- Ensure adequate administrative support
- "Supervision is a distinct specialty area and deserves recognition as such"
- Legitimization of the field of supervision
- Increase interest in competency articulation and development through appropriate methodology
- Competency-based approach to supervision complements educational model goals and readily supports empirical study

**Superordinate values informing both psychotherapy and supervision:**
1. Integrity-in-relationship
2. Ethical values-based practice
3. Appreciation for diversity
4. Science-informed practice

**Level of commitment required:**
- Initiatives to improve supervisory practice require commitment at EACH level in the system: institution, clinical faculty, and individual supervisor
- "Programs of development require tailoring to the specific needs and unique characteristics of a particular training clinic, hospital, agency, program, or university"
- "Such initiatives taken at the individual or institutional level reflect professional values and commitment to competent supervision practice"

### 2. Practice Improvement Models (PIMs)

**Cape and Barkham (2002) — Methods for improving clinical practice, appropriated for supervision enhancement:**

1. **Training workshops** — particularly those using experiential rather than just didactic methods; straightforward approach to training and professional development
2. **Clinical supervision (supervision of supervision)**
3. **Clinical guidelines / supervision guidelines** — tools to orient process to particularly salient content areas (cultural/diversity competency). "Focus attention on areas of agreement about appropriate practice, whether based on research evidence or consensus"
4. **Evidence-based clinical methods**
5. **Practice-based methods** — focused directly on practices of the clinician/supervisor. Implementation of supervisor self-report measures in conjunction with supervision guidelines and objective analysis of supervisor behavior (Milne & James, 2002, competency-based model)
6. **Clinical audit**
7. **Outcomes monitoring and management** — directly related to assessment of supervisory outcomes. Assessment of outcomes can be conducted by observing skills demonstrated in therapy sessions and through client response measures (Lambert & Hawkins, 2001)
8. **Outcomes benchmarking**
9. **Continuous quality improvement**

Using multiple PIMs is more likely to affect supervisor behavior and supervisee/patient outcomes than use of a singular intervention strategy.

### 3. Technology in Supervision (Tele-Supervision)

- On-line supervision presents unique challenges in confidentiality and informed consent "because of the loss of objective control over transmitted data" (Kanz, 2001; mentioned in Chapter 7)
- With adoption of HIPAA privacy rule, any health-care provider who transmits protected health information in electronic form must be HIPAA compliant
- Implications of HIPAA for training settings are just beginning to be explored

### 4. Trends and Innovations

**Competency-based benchmarks:**
- Levels of competence can be operationalized and specific benchmarks of behavior defined
- Supervisor effectiveness can be evaluated, in part, by benchmarks achieved by the supervisee during training
- Complements competency-based approach: specific components identified, training goals defined, procedures for assessing performance objectives established

**Ongoing peer supervision and review:**
- Per APA's Ethical Guidelines for Clinical Supervisors (ACES, 1995)
- Provides means for formative feedback
- Offers learning opportunities for each participant

**Provision of ongoing supervision and support for ALL levels of supervisors:**
- An intervention that will strengthen practice

**Self-assessment advocacy:**
- Individuals and programs should self-assess their competence at supervision in general and in each prescribed area (personal factors, diversity, ethics and legal, evaluation)
- Begin to build on existing structures to enhance program functioning

**Client-outcome-based supervision evaluation:**
- Currently, most assessment procedures rely on supervisee self-reports of competence and satisfaction — do not actually measure outcomes in client progress or development of clinical competencies
- Movement toward observing skills demonstrated in therapy sessions and client response measures

**Movement toward standardization in evaluation:**
- From "homemade" measures lacking reliability/validity toward criterion measures
- Use of rubrics, behavioral anchors, Likert scales
- Multisource (360-degree) evaluation

### 5. How Supervision Practice Evolves Across Career Stages

The chapter discusses:
- "The requisite competencies to perform supervision will continue to evolve in step with developments in professional psychology, the needs of an increasingly diverse populace, continuing changes in the nature of service delivery, and the requirements of accountability in assessing the competence of supervisees and outcomes of their clients"
- Supervision is at the center of these developments
- "No matter how high the present quality of clinical training and supervision provided... competence, by definition, can always be enhanced"
- Individual supervisors and their institutions have both opportunity and responsibility for continuous improvement in quality (Nelson, Batalden, & Ryer, 1998)
- Ongoing development of competence: as individuals (Chapter 3), in diversity (Chapter 6), and in ethics and legal applications (Chapter 7)

### 6. Mentoring vs. Supervision Distinction

This specific distinction is not explicitly detailed as a separate section in Chapter 9. However, the chapter's broader context distinguishes:

**Supervision:**
- A distinct specialty requiring education and training
- Has evaluative and gatekeeping functions
- Involves formal contracts, documentation, and due process
- Carries legal liability (vicarious liability)
- Requires adherence to ethical codes and standards of care
- Goal: ensure competent client care and supervisee development

**Mentoring:**
- Referenced indirectly through discussion of multiple roles
- Biaggio, Paget, and Chenoweth (1997) described mentoring as one of the overlapping social, mentoring, and professional relationships that are normative in training departments — which can be problematic as potential dual relationships
- Does not carry the same evaluative power, legal liability, or formal gatekeeping responsibilities

The book overall elevates supervision as a formal, competency-based professional practice distinct from both therapy and informal mentoring.

### 7. Competencies for the Evolving Practice of Clinical Supervision

(From the overall framework of Chapter 9 and the book's conclusion):

- Knowledge and skills of a clinician, educator, supervisor, and consultant
- Personal and interpersonal qualities
- Ability to self-assess competence at supervision in general and in each prescribed area (personal factors, diversity, ethics/legal, evaluation)
- Commitment to continuous improvement in quality
- Ability to use practice improvement models (PIMs)
- Ability to measure outcomes (both supervisee development and client progress)
- Ability to operationalize levels of competence and define specific behavioral benchmarks
- Receptivity to feedback (from supervisees, peers, and meta-supervisors)
- Willingness to engage in ongoing peer supervision and review
- Ability to provide supervision and support for all levels of supervisors
- Tailoring of programs to specific needs and unique characteristics of local setting
